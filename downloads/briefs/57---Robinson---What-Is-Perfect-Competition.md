# 57 · Robinson (1934) — What Is Perfect Competition?
*Quarterly Journal of Economics, 49(1), pp. 104–120* · Group: E — Imperfect vs monopolistic competition · Tier: 1

**Hook (memorize this line):** Perfect competition just means a perfectly elastic demand curve for each seller. Free entry and normal profits are separate questions.

**Responds to:** Knight, Risk, Uncertainty and Profit (wide definition, context); Sraffa 1926 (context, not in folder); Shove 1930 (#38) and Shove, EJ March 1933 (cf. #47); Chamberlin's "pure vs perfect" competition (1933 book, context); Harrod 1934 (#56); Cournot's large-numbers condition  
**Answered by:** #63 Chamberlin 1937. He agrees that restriction of entry is compatible with perfect competition (p. 566), but rebuts her product-differentiation point and all four of her references to him (pp. 568–569 n.). He also reads the whole article as proof that she keeps monopoly and competition separate (p. 573).

## Main claim
Define perfect competition narrowly: the demand for an individual seller's output is perfectly elastic. This must be kept apart from free entry and normal profits, which are "analytically quite distinct." Perfect competition needs (1) a perfect market and (2) enough firms. But the number of firms required depends on the slope of their marginal cost curves, and "in some cases, may be quite small."

## Argument in steps
1. **Definition.** She rejects Knight's catch-all definition. She also rejects Chamberlin's "pure/perfect" split as paying "a verbal tribute to the old confusion" and instead calls perfect competition what he calls "pure" competition. Harrod shares her definition but slips in free entry (pp. 104–106).
2. **Normal profits** are "simply the supply price of entrepreneurship to a particular industry." They differ between industries. High normal profits are linked to imperfect markets only empirically, through goodwill. Following Shove there are two levels, one to attract entrants and one to keep existing firms, which gives an upper and a lower long-period supply curve (pp. 106–111).
3. **Perfect market.** Product differentiation is neither necessary nor sufficient. If all buyers pay exactly 6d. more for A than for B and each buys only one of them, demand for each is still perfectly elastic. A market is perfect when all buyers have the same preferences *and* each deals with only one firm at a time. One buyer means a necessarily perfect market. Many buyers make imperfection *more* likely (pp. 112–114).
4. **Number of firms.** Cournot's test (removing a firm leaves price unchanged) is "much too severe." Her own 1933 book said that a small firm's marginal revenue is close to price. Now she goes further: when one firm adds a unit, rival firms (setting MC = price) cut output, so industry output rises by less than one unit (pp. 115–117).
5. **Marginal cost slopes.** With falling MC only one firm can survive. Constant MC gives perfect competition with two firms, but this cannot last in the long run. With rising MC, the number of firms needed is smaller the flatter the MC curves. With vertical MC it must be "indefinitely great," but then perfection does not matter: the Saturday-night fish market still sells the competitive output (pp. 118–120).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Perfect competition (Robinson)** — the demand for the individual seller's output is perfectly elastic. Nothing about entry.
- **Normal profits** — the level at which the number of firms has no tendency to change, i.e. the supply price of entrepreneurship to an industry.
- **Perfect market** — a rise in one firm's price (others unchanged) ends its sales completely. This needs identical buyer preferences and each buyer dealing with one firm at a time.
- **Quasi-long-period supply curve** — the supply curve of a fixed number of firms (from her 1933 book).
- **Don't confuse:** Robinson's "perfect" = Chamberlin's "pure." Chamberlin's "perfect" adds further perfections such as mobility and certainty.

## Diagram
Two long-period supply curves (upper for expansions, lower for contractions), linked by quasi-long-period curves for a fixed number of firms (p. 110):
```
price |        upper LP S (entry)
      |      /
      |  R  /      quasi-LP curve (fixed no. of firms)
      |   |/  P----joins upper and lower curves
      |   S\ 
      |     \ lower LP S (exit)
      +------------------ output
```
The key result: if equilibrium is reached gradually, the *pair* of curves is as determinate as the old single curve.
