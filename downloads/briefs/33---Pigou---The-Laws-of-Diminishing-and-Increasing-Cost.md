# 33 · A. C. Pigou (1927) — The Laws of Diminishing and Increasing Cost
*Economic Journal, 37(146), Jun 1927, pp. 188–197* · Group: D1 Pigou's welfare economics & the cost controversy to 1928 · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** Pigou accepts Sraffa: no increasing costs; but tiny external economies can still add up to decreasing costs.

**Responds to:** Sraffa, "The Laws of Returns under Competitive Conditions," EJ Dec 1926 (context, not in your folder)  
**Answered by:** Pigou's own fuller version #35 (1928); #37 Young 1928; the 1930 symposium #38 (group D2)

## Main claim
For a commodity that uses only a small share of each factor, so that factor prices are unaffected, increasing costs are impossible: output can always be raised 10% by raising every factor 10%. Here Pigou agrees with Sraffa. But he rejects Sraffa's claim that external economies special to one industry are negligible. What matters is the *ratio* of the cost saving to the output increase, and two second-order quantities can have a first-order ratio. So decreasing costs are compatible with competition, and output there falls short of the social optimum unless the State gives a bounty.

## Argument in steps
1. The cost function is the long-period, "normal now" link between output and aggregate cost. It counts only changes *due to* the scale of output, and excludes spontaneous inventions and changes in taste (pp. 188–189).
2. Costs can only be measured in a common unit if relative factor values stay fixed. So the study is limited to industries too small to affect factor prices (pp. 189–192).
3. Average cost f(x)/x and marginal cost f′(x) can move differently, so say "increasing/decreasing average" or "marginal" costs; "constant costs" is unambiguous (pp. 192–193).
4. Increasing costs are impossible for such industries (agrees with Sraffa). For a monopoly, internal economies come in two sorts: gradual (specialisation, invention) and "minimum apparatus" (medals, a printed book, rail: £100,000 for the first hundredweight, 6d for each one after) (pp. 193–195).
5. Under competition the representative firm has an optimum size, so only external economies remain. Example: £100,000 for 100,000 units, £175,000 for 200,000 units. The saving from one extra unit is ·0012 of a penny, yet doubling output cuts average cost 12½%. External economies arrive gradually, like new buyers as price falls (pp. 195–196). Supply curve = average-cost curve when marginal cost is below it. The optimum is where demand = marginal cost, so decreasing-cost industries under-produce; State bounty (p. 197).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Cost function (normal, now)** — aggregate cost of each output after full adaptation, in today's state of knowledge, counting only changes caused by scale.
- **Average vs marginal costs** — can slope differently where the curve turns. Hence the need to say which.
- **Second-order quantities, first-order ratio** — Pigou's reply to Sraffa on external economies.
- **Don't confuse:** Pigou *agrees* with Sraffa on increasing costs and *disagrees* on decreasing costs. Do not swap them.
