## Main claim
Robinson defines normal profit as the rate that neither attracts nor repels firms. Under imperfect competition that gives only a *range* of rates. Harrod picks one rate: the rate just enough to make the firm itself add fixed capital. This rate holds at the *margin* of the firm's plant. It need not hold on average, so price need not equal average total cost, and tangency is not required. Hence an imperfect-competition firm *may* have decreasing costs in equilibrium. It need not.

## Argument in steps
1. A unique rate that attracts entrants above it and drives firms out below it "should surely" mean perfect competition. So Robinson only defines limits (pp. 337–338).
2. Harrod's normal rate: the return that induces the firm to add fixed equipment. It is likely above the exit rate and below the entry rate, and it is the supply price of fixed equipment (p. 338).
3. Long-period cost = the envelope of the plant parabolas (from #43), with each built on this normal rate. In equilibrium the *marginal* plant earns the normal rate (p. 339).
4. Tangency of the demand curve to the cost curve would need average profit = marginal = normal. That is not necessary. If price > average total cost, the long-period cost curve "may not have a negative gradient at all". After a long depression, decreasing costs become more likely (p. 340).
5. Result: decreasing costs *may* occur in equilibrium (as in 1931). That conflicts with Robinson's *must*. If the shifted demand curve keeps constant elasticity, the supply price curve and the cost curve slope the same way (pp. 340–341).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Normal profit (Harrod 1933)** — the rate on the marginal installation that just induces the firm to extend its plant.
- **Marginal revenue curve** — Harrod's footnote says Robinson "re-christened" his "increment of aggregate demand curve" (p. 338 n.).
- **Don't confuse:** Robinson's normal profit is set by *entry*. Harrod's is set by the firm's own *investment at the margin*. And note: the article starts partway down PDF p. 2, after the end of another piece (Somerville).
