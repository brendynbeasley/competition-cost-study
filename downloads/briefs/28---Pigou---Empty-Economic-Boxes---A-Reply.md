# 28 · A. C. Pigou (1922) — Empty Economic Boxes: A Reply
*Economic Journal, 32(128), Dec 1922, pp. 458–465* · Group: D1 Pigou's welfare economics & the cost controversy to 1928 · Tier: 1

**Hook (memorize this line):** Pigou to Clapham: the boxes hold "little cases" of elasticities, which are useful, and fillable with statistics.

**Responds to:** #27 Clapham 1922  
**Answered by:** Clapham's rejoinder, Notes and Memoranda in the same issue (context, not in your folder); #31 Robertson 1924 (who leaves the methods quarrel aside and attacks Pigou's apparatus)

## Main claim
The categories of increasing and diminishing returns are not detachable boxes. They are working parts of the whole analysis of value, and they can be useful even while empty, by exposing hidden assumptions. What practice really needs is the "little cases" inside the boxes: numerical elasticities of supply and demand. Filling them is hard but not impossible, and needs theorists, business experts and statisticians working together.

## Argument in steps
1. Restates Clapham as four propositions: returns are hard to conceive; the boxes are hard to fill; filled boxes would do little good; so abolish them. Pigou grants only the first, and says it is old news (p. 458).
2. Clapham blurs "pure" knowledge of implications with realistic knowledge. Knowledge of the form "If X, then Y" is no worse than "In the year 1600, X" (the king and the lampreys). But Pigou himself values economics for practical fruit (pp. 459–461).
3. The returns categories are "elements in the intellectual machinery" of value theory; dropping them would wreck the analysis of how output and cost relate (pp. 460–462).
4. Even empty, they help in a negative way. Anyone who says a tax always raises price by the full tax is quietly assuming constant returns (p. 462).
5. If filled, the big "valises" (increasing vs diminishing) tell little, like knowing a temperature is above normal. The "little cases" (elasticities near current output) would guide tax policy. Clapham's "other considerations" objection is like refusing to check which suit fits because price matters too (pp. 462–464). Filling the cases needs business experts and statistics: train more economists like Jevons (pp. 464–465).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Valises vs little cases** — broad categories of returns versus precise numerical ranges for elasticities of supply (η) and demand (e), the latter being what policy needs.
- **Pure vs realistic knowledge** — knowledge of implications (logic, mathematics) versus knowledge of actual subject-matter.
- **Negative use of theory** — exposing the hidden assumptions behind popular claims.
- **Don't confuse:** Pigou does *not* defend pure theory for its own sake; he says plainly that practical usefulness is what he looks for (p. 461).
