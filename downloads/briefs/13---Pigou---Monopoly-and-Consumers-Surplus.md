## Main claim
Some point on the "total benefit" curve always lies above the monopolist's maximum revenue. So a monopolist can gain by attacking consumers' surplus, in two ways:
1. **Directly**: by price discrimination, or by refusing to sell except in parcels of a fixed size.
2. **Indirectly**: by threatening to withhold supply, which drives out would-be competitors.

The parcels device needs four conditions that will rarely hold together.

## Argument in steps
1. **Perfect and group discrimination (pp. 388–389).** Ideally the monopolist charges a separate price for each unit, matching the intensity of demand, and takes the whole surplus. That is impracticable. Group discrimination does happen where goods are "difficult to transfer between persons and through time". Examples: gas and water companies, and railways raising the charge per pound-mile when a customer's demand is strong.
2. **Selling in parcels (p. 389).** The monopolist refuses to sell except in parcels of a set size. This forces the buyer off his demand curve and onto the indifference curve through the origin (Edgeworth). On curve DB, area PQF equals area DNF, so buying OM at price PM leaves the buyer no surplus.
3. **Many buyers (p. 390).** With one buyer facing one seller, this is an indeterminate bargain. With many independent buyers, refusing to buy is a sure sacrifice for each of them, so the monopolist can succeed.
4. **Four conditions (pp. 390–392).** All four together are improbable:
   1. no combination among buyers;
   2. imperfect transferability (otherwise middlemen split the parcels);
   3. perishability (otherwise buyers just buy larger lots less often);
   4. buyers fall into a few small groups of similar people.
5. **Driving out rivals (pp. 392–394).** A combination can threaten to cut off a customer who deals with a rival, provided area DKQ exceeds area DRP. Examples: factors' agreements and "unionised" shops; shipping conferences offering more frequent sailings; telephone companies; leases of patented shoe machinery tied to the owner's other machines.

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Consumers' surplus**: total benefit minus payment (Marshall).
- **Discrimination**: charging different groups different prices. It requires goods that cannot easily be resold or shifted in time.
- **Selling in parcels**: setting a minimum lot size larger than the buyer would choose. Effectively an all-or-nothing offer.
- **Indifference curve through the origin**: Edgeworth's curve along which the buyer's net gain is zero.
- **Extruding competitors**: using power over buyers' surplus to exclude rivals (boycotts, tied leases).
- **Don't confuse:** the vocabulary of first, second and third degree is *not* in this 1904 paper; it comes from *Wealth and Welfare* (1912) (context). Rough equivalents: perfect discrimination ≈ the later first degree; group discrimination ≈ the third degree.
