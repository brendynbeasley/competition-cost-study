# 59 · Kahn (1935) — Some Notes on Ideal Output
*Economic Journal, 45(177), pp. 1–35* · Group: E — Imperfect vs monopolistic competition · Tier: 1

**Hook (memorize this line):** What matters is deviation from the average. If every industry were equally imperfect, there would be nothing to correct.

**Responds to:** Pigou, Economics of Welfare (4th ed. 1932) on marginal social vs private net product (context, not in folder); Shove 1928, "Varying Costs and Marginal Net Products" (#36); Robinson, EIC ch. 27 "A World of Monopolies" and #50 (Euler); Clapham's "empty boxes" (#27); Young 1913 (#26); Knight 1924 (#30); Edgeworth 1925 (#21); Kaldor #49 and #53; Lerner, Fraser, Viner (context)  
**Answered by:** #63 Chamberlin 1937 quotes Kahn p. 20 ("imperfection of competition does not carry … implications … [of] monopoly") as proof that the English school keeps monopoly and competition apart. He also attacks the negative-marginal-product-of-entrepreneur argument (pp. 575, 579)

## Main claim
Output is ideal when the value of the marginal social product of each factor is equal in all uses. Whether the distortion comes from external economies or from imperfect competition, the case for intervention depends on an industry's *deviation from the average*, not on the absolute amount. Pigou's rules hold only if divergences are rare. Under imperfect competition, hired factors should move *into* abnormally imperfect industries, while entrepreneurs (firms) should move *out*: more workers and fewer shops.

## Argument in steps
1. **Set-up (Pigou's method).** Full employment; money as a measure of utility (income treated as equally distributed); the "ideal" amount of a factor in a use is where the values of marginal social product are equal across uses (pp. 1–4).
2. **External economies.** Rank industries by external economies. Expand those above the average and contract those below. Pigou's contrary rule (expand any industry with positive external economies) is valid only on his tacit assumption that external economies are rare (the "archetypal industry") (pp. 5–9). A falling supply curve is only mildly correlated with a case for a bounty (pp. 9–11).
3. **Lumpy units.** External economies usually come from the internal economies of subsidiary firms working "lumpy" units below capacity, which happens under imperfect competition. So subsidise the railway or utility until price equals marginal cost. "Dr. Clapham's economic boxes were full all the time, but the economists were looking on the wrong shelf" (pp. 11–14). Smoke and road congestion: roads are a scarce factor; charge the toll that competing private owners would charge (pp. 15–19).
4. **Imperfect competition and hired factors.** A factor earns MPP × MR, and MR/price = (e − 1)/e. So expand industries where competition is more imperfect than the average and contract the rest; "If competition were everywhere equally imperfect there would be no case for interference" (pp. 19–22).
5. **Entrepreneurs.** Under imperfect competition the entrepreneur earns *more* than the value of his marginal physical product (following Robinson #50). In the irrational-preference case: marginal social productivity = earnings − (cost of hired factors)/(e − 1). So divert entrepreneurs *away* from abnormally imperfect industries (retail: more hired factors, fewer shops). Regulate with a subsidy on hired factors plus a lump-sum tax per entrepreneur, which pay for each other. If the average cost of hired factors is falling, the entrepreneur's marginal product is negative and there are too many firms. If preferences are "rational," add the extra revenue the firm would get under perfect discrimination (pp. 22–35).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Ideal output** — output at which the value of the marginal social product of each factor is the same in every use.
- **Rational vs irrational imperfection** — rational preferences reflect real satisfactions (transport costs, genuine tastes). Irrational ones come from ignorance or inertia and vanish if the consumer is moved to another firm.
- **Imperfect competition (Kahn)** — the firm's demand is not perfectly elastic. Elasticity is an inverse measure of the degree of imperfection, with *no* connotation of monopoly in the traditional sense (p. 20).
- **Don't confuse:** hired factors and entrepreneurs should be moved in *opposite* directions (irrational case, same-industry factor competition). Also, Kahn's ideal output is relative to the average, not to perfect competition.
