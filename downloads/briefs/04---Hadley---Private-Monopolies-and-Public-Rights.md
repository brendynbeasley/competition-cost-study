# 04 · Hadley (1886) — Private Monopolies and Public Rights
*Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1(1), Oct 1886, pp. 28–44* · Group: A — Trusts, monopoly & the state · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** Railways teach the rule for all big-capital industry: competition ruins, so accept combination, but regulate it.

**Responds to:** Traditional transport law (public highway, private carrier); Hudson's *The Railways and the Republic*; Henry George vs Hyndman on land and capital; the US Supreme Court's Granger Cases. Takes the "law of the increasing return" from H. C. Adams (#07; Hadley credits the phrase to him, p. 40)  
**Answered by:** #06 Giddings 1887 (footnotes this article as an example of economists who predict competition will be suppressed and want compulsory publicity, p. 65); #05 Clark 1887 reaches a parallel "accept and regulate" view; #12 Bullock 1901 lists Hadley's later writings among those who blame trusts on destructive competition

## Main claim
Where an industry has large fixed investment, as railways first showed, free competition is unstable. It either cannot happen or it drives rates below cost until the firms consolidate. The state should therefore stop forcing competition. Instead it should accept combination and control it through equality of treatment and compulsory publicity. Hadley predicts that factory industry will follow the railway path.

## Argument in steps
1. Old law kept the highway public (a natural monopoly) and left the carrier private and competitive. The railway is highway and carrier at once, so the old split broke down (pp. 29–31).
2. Four possible fixes (p. 31): (1) keep road and carrier separate; (2) state monopoly; (3) parallel competing private roads; (4) admit monopoly, but keep public rights of control. Fix (1) fails because the railway itself can always undercut outside carriers (pp. 32–33).
3. Fix (3), competing highways, fails (pp. 34–36). A parallel road is often physically impossible and always wasteful. Fixed charges get ignored in a rate war, just as rent does not enter the price of wheat. Railways are not subject to diminishing return, so "there is thus no direct limit to this cut-throat competition" (p. 35). The war ends in consolidation, with discrimination and unstable rates along the way.
4. Fixing rates directly (the Granger laws) also failed. The better tools are equal treatment of shippers and publicity of management; "regulated combination is better than irresponsible competition" (pp. 37–39).
5. The same holds in any industry with large permanent investment, i.e. Adams's increasing return. Price wars alternate with monopoly prices, and the cycle ends in combination: Standard Oil, coal, steel rails (pp. 39–41). The Granger "public use"/"virtual monopoly" doctrine could reach factories, and US railway law went through three phases: forced competition, shipper dictation, regulated combination with publicity. Factory law may be next (pp. 42–44).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Highway vs carrier**: the road (natural monopoly, publicly controlled) vs the business of carrying (competitive). The railway merges the two.
- **Fixed charges vs transportation (operating) expenses**: in a fight for traffic, any rate above operating expenses pays, so competitive rates fall toward operating cost and leave nothing for interest.
- **Monopoly from capital organization**: Hadley's third type, after legal monopoly (largely past) and natural monopoly (e.g. land). He says it is the type "characteristic of the present day" (p. 40).
- **Equality and publicity**: his remedies, in place of rate-fixing or state ownership.
- **Don't confuse:** Hadley does *not* recommend state ownership (he rejects the socialist inference, p. 43). Nor does he recommend legislated rates (the Granger laws "proved so bad," p. 37). He wants *regulated private combination*.

## Diagram
None central. Hadley argues the cost logic in words. In a rate war the price floor is operating (transportation) expenses, not full cost including fixed charges. With no diminishing return, extra traffic costs less and less, so the war has no natural stopping point.

## One line to quote
> "regulated combination is better than irresponsible competition." (p. 39)

## Likely exam angle
- An early statement of "ruinous competition" in fixed-cost industries, and of the case for accepting and regulating combination rather than banning it. Compare with Clark #05 (agrees) and Giddings #06 (doubts that competition can really be suppressed).
- Use him as a link to the railway-rates group (fixed vs variable cost, discrimination) and to Adams's increasing-returns test for state control.
