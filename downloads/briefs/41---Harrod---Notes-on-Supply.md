## Main claim
In a single "source" of supply, costs fall at first and then rise. So under perfect competition output is set where rising marginal cost meets a horizontal demand line. Where the source faces a falling demand curve, output is set where marginal cost cuts a new curve derived from demand, the *increment of aggregate demand curve*. Then decreasing supply price becomes possible. Separately, an industry that uses factors in unusual proportions faces increasing supply price, which explains why agriculture was treated as a diminishing-returns industry.

## Argument in steps
1. **Section I, short period:** with fixed equipment, marginal prime cost (KCm) cuts average prime cost (KCp) at its minimum E and average total cost (LCt) at its minimum F, the "optimum output" (p. 233). Under perfect competition output is where MC meets the horizontal demand line on the rising part: increasing supply price (p. 234).
2. A source "working under capacity" (could cut average prime costs by expanding) is evidence of "quasi-monopoly" (p. 235). Long period: some fixed equipment and goodwill outlast period *s*. So a fall in demand raises short-period cost schedules: the "law of decreasing cost conditions". It is asymmetric, because permanent equipment can be increased but not decreased (pp. 236–238).
3. **Section II:** a source that is not small faces a falling demand curve DD′. From it Harrod derives DD″, which shows the price of *x* units minus the price of *x*−1 units. Output is where MC cuts DD″ (pp. 238–239). Marshall's "net product" must be valued this way. Under perfect competition DD′ and DD″ coincide.
4. So a monopolistic source can show decreasing supply price, in the short period if it has excess capacity and in the long period through larger plant. With a few sources, "all sources may" show decreasing supply prices (pp. 239–240).
5. **Section III:** an industry that uses a factor in more than the national-average proportion (a+x : b : c) can only get it at a rising price. So it has increasing supply price, and more so the bigger the industry. With land as factor A this explains classical agriculture better than "inelasticity of land" does (pp. 240–241).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Increment of aggregate demand curve** — DD″: total receipts from *x* units minus total receipts from *x*−1. This is the marginal revenue curve in all but name.
- **Source of supply** — a unit (firm or plant) within which costs split into prime and supplementary.
- **Law of decreasing cost conditions** — short-period cost schedules rise after demand falls, because fixed equipment is not renewed.
- **Don't confuse:** Harrod does *not* use the phrase "marginal revenue". Robinson 1932 coined that name, and Harrod 1933 accepts it. Also, Section III's "increasing supply price" comes from factor prices, not from the cost curves of the firm.
