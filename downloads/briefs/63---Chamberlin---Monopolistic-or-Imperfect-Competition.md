# 63 · Chamberlin (1937) — Monopolistic or Imperfect Competition?
*Quarterly Journal of Economics, 51(4), pp. 557–580* · Group: E — Imperfect vs monopolistic competition · Tier: 1

**Hook (memorize this line):** Monopolistic competition is a blend of monopoly and competition, while "imperfect" competition keeps the old dichotomy.

**Responds to:** Robinson, Economics of Imperfect Competition 1933 (context) and #57; Kaldor #58; Kahn #59; Harrod #61; Robinson #50 (exploitation, entrepreneur); J. M. Clark; White 1936; Machlup's 1937 remarks (context)  
**Answered by:** #66 Kaldor 1938. Chamberlin's further reply (#67) is not in folder (context). Machlup #69 later uses the "narrow sense" (product differentiation) and the wider sense of the term.

## Main claim
Part I clears away six "misconceptions" about this type of theory. Part II argues that monopolistic competition differs *in conception* from Robinson's imperfect competition. Chamberlin sees most situations as "composites of both competition and monopoly." Robinson, who defines a monopolist as any individual seller, keeps monopoly and competition mutually exclusive. The difference matters for profits, for "competitive" norms (pure competition is no ideal), and for "exploitation."

## Argument in steps
1. **Six misconceptions** (pp. 558–570):
   - *MR curve*: MR = MC is just profit maximisation and holds even under pure competition. Robinson exaggerates the curve's importance.
   - *Increasing returns*: these are a *result* of monopolistic competition, not part of its definition. They are "neither necessary nor sufficient."
   - *Tangency*: tangency is "mainly an expositional device" resting on "heroic assumptions" (book pp. 82, 110 ff.). With or without tangency there is a blend of monopoly and competition, and the whole theory of monopoly is part of monopolistic competition.
   - *Numbers*: more firms need not flatten demand curves. New stores may expand the area; menthol cigarettes are not "in between" brands; on a line the slope stays the same.
   - *Divisibility*: Kaldor's claim that perfect divisibility gives pure competition implies "an infinite number of infinitesimally small firms."
   - *Entry*: he records a change of view. Following Kaldor, full free entry means producing the identical product, which is "quite incompatible with a differentiated product." Entry means only freedom to produce substitutes.
   - *Differentiation*: Robinson's counter-example (same goods, different firm names) is itself differentiation.
2. **Nature of monopolistic competition** (pp. 570–573). Chapter IV ("Differentiation of the Product") is a new *Weltanschauung*, not a technique. Each producer has a monopoly of his own variety but faces the competition of substitutes, so monopolistic competition "embraces the whole theory of monopoly." "Imperfect competition" is a "purely negative" term that keeps the categories mutually exclusive.
3. **Contrast with Robinson** (pp. 573–576). She defines a monopolist as any individual seller (like calling "either rail of a railway track … a monorail"). Barring that definition "there is no monopoly whatever" in her imperfect competition. Kahn (#59 p. 20) says so explicitly.
4. **Consequences** (pp. 576–580):
   - *Profits*: for Robinson, profits inside an arbitrary "industry" are all competitive. For Chamberlin, true monopoly profits are spread throughout the system.
   - *Norms*: free enterprise produces monopolistic competition, product variety and advertising, so pure competition is "no longer … an 'ideal'".
   - *Exploitation*: *all* factors get less than MPP × price, not only labour. Robinson's entrepreneur-as-exploiter depends on treating the entrepreneur as one indivisible unit equal to the firm. Entrepreneurship is in fact divisible.

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Monopolistic competition** — a blend of monopoly and competition grounded in product differentiation, real or fancied. It includes selling outlays and product variation.
- **Pure vs perfect competition** — pure means no monopoly element (perfectly elastic firm demand). Perfect adds further perfections. (Robinson #57 rejects this terminology.)
- **Large-group tangency** — demand tangent to average cost, so no monopoly profit. Here demoted to an expository device.
- **Don't confuse:** "imperfect competition" in Robinson's sense ≠ "monopolistic competition." Chamberlin insists the difference is conceptual, not terminological. Kaldor (#66) says it is just terminology.
