## Main claim
Robbins gave this as a lecture in Vienna (7 April 1933, p. 1 n.). Costs are the *value* of displaced alternatives (Wieser's Law), not technical quantities of goods foregone. From a position of equilibrium, cost must rise. Marshall's constant and falling supply curves do not contradict this, because they answer a different question: how one equilibrium differs from another after the data change. They are rough "schemata" and can mislead about the system as a whole. Falling costs under competition come from exploiting *indivisible* potential uses of factors (Young's division of labour), which is a many-industry, disintegrating process that supply curves cannot show.

## Argument in steps
1. **Part I:** cost is "what must be surrendered". He rejects Knight's and Haberler's purely technical "displacement" cost ("the cost of beaver is deer"). It fails with fixed coefficients and across products made from different factors, and it ignores the valuations entrepreneurs actually face (pp. 2–6).
2. **Part II:** Wieser's Law implies rising cost at equilibrium if other things stay equal. Constant and falling supply curves are "essentially germane to a Theory of Variations": when demand shifts, factors are released elsewhere. So there is no contradiction (pp. 6–8).
3. Supply curves are schemata, like Edgeworth's clock-hands, with limited validity for final equilibrium. They mislead on economy-wide effects (the monopoly example). Young's critique of Pigou shows the danger, and marginal productivity (general equilibrium) is safer (pp. 8–11).
4. **Part III:** under competition, technical cost reductions must come from external economies. They wait on market size because of *indivisibility*. Competition rules out indivisibility in *actual* uses but not in *potential* uses (the gifted marketer; machines and transport as capital accumulates) (pp. 11–14).
5. This is Young's theory: falling costs come from many industries and from vertical disintegration, both "incompatible with the implications of the supply curve" (pp. 14–15). **Part IV:** short and long period are sections of one process. What counts as prime cost depends on *expectations*, so cost theory must join trade-cycle theory (pp. 15–18).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Wieser's Law** — the cost of a thing reflects the value of the alternatives displaced (opportunity cost).
- **Theory of Variations** — comparison of equilibria after the data change (comparative statics), as opposed to the conditions *of* equilibrium.
- **Actual vs potential uses of factors** — indivisible potential uses become profitable only as the system expands.
- **Don't confuse:** this is Robbins *1934* on cost theory, not Robbins *1928* (#34) on the Representative Firm. Also, he does *not* deny falling supply curves. He reclassifies them.
