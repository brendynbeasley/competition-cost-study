# 49 · Kaldor (1934) — The Equilibrium of the Firm
*Economic Journal, 44(173), pp. 60–76* · Group: E — Imperfect vs monopolistic competition · Tier: 1

**Hook (memorize this line):** Co-ordination is the firm's only fixed factor — and in static equilibrium it becomes a free good.

**Responds to:** Marshall's representative firm (#08 Marshall 1890); Robbins on the representative firm (#34); Robertson 1930 symposium (#38); Pigou's "equilibrium firm" (Economics of Welfare, context); Harrod on decreasing costs, EJ June 1933 (cf. #45); Chamberlin's and Robinson's 1933 books (context)  
**Answered by:** Kaldor himself builds on it in #58 (p. 36 n.); cited by Robinson #50 (p. 406 n.) and Kahn #59 (p. 17 n.). Coase's "Nature of the Firm" (Nov 1937) takes up the same question (context, not in your folder; #64 is a different Coase paper)

## Main claim
A Marshallian supply curve needs perfect competition plus a determinate long-run cost curve for each firm. The only thing that can limit firm size in the long run is a fixed factor, and the only candidate is *co-ordinating ability*. But co-ordination is needed only to adjust to change, so in static equilibrium the firm's optimum size becomes infinite or indeterminate. Long-period static equilibrium and perfect competition are therefore incompatible.

## Argument in steps
1. Writers since Marshall assume cost curves that fit the industry's supply curve. They make the firm's equilibrium depend on the industry's, when it should be the other way round (pp. 61–63).
2. Under perfect competition the firm's long-run cost curve must eventually rise. Indivisibilities cannot do this: once all factors are variable, costs fall back to the same minimum. External diseconomies cannot do it either, because they hit all firms equally. So there must be a factor fixed for the firm but in elastic supply to the industry (pp. 65–67).
3. "Entrepreneurship" has three meanings. Uncertainty-bearing is not fixed (joint-stock companies spread it). Supervision is not fixed (you can double the foremen). *Co-ordination* is fixed, because every decision must be compared with all others and "pass through a single brain." So a firm is a "productive combination possessing a given unit of co-ordinating ability" (pp. 67–69).
4. Co-ordination is a dynamic function, a feature of "disequilibrium." As the firm adjusts, co-ordinating tasks shrink. In a stationary state co-ordination is a free good and the optimum size of the firm is infinite or indeterminate (pp. 70–71).
5. Conclusions: there is a continual tendency for firms to grow, so static equilibrium and perfect competition are inconsistent. Under imperfect competition, demand and supply curves limit size, but the cost curve stays partly indeterminate. In practice the effect is "insignificant" next to monetary instability (pp. 72–76).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Co-ordination vs supervision** — supervision checks that contracts are carried out and can be divided among many supervisors. Co-ordination decides which contracts to make and cannot be divided.
- **Fixed vs indivisible factor** — fixity of supply ≠ indivisibility (p. 64 n.). Co-ordinating ability is fixed but need not be indivisible (p. 73).
- **Perfect competition (Kaldor 1934)** — "all prices are given to the individual firm" (p. 60 n.).
- **Don't confuse:** this is a cost-side argument about the *limit to firm size*, not the excess-capacity critique (that is #58). Also not Coase's "Nature of the Firm."
