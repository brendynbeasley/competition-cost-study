# 66 · Kaldor (1938) — Professor Chamberlin on Monopolistic and Imperfect Competition
*Quarterly Journal of Economics, 52(3), pp. 513–529* · Group: E — Imperfect vs monopolistic competition · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** Chamberlin "is trying to differentiate his product too far": one subject, and elasticity, not slope, measures imperfection.

**Responds to:** #63 Chamberlin 1937; defends his own #58 (1935); Cassels, QJE May 1937 (context); Robinson's book (context)  
**Answered by:** Chamberlin's reply (#67, context, not in folder)

## Main claim
Chamberlin's "dissimilarities" are really misconceptions. The two theories have the same subject matter. MR matters because the degree of imperfection is the gap between price and marginal cost, which depends on elasticity. More firms in a given section of the field *raise elasticity*, even if curves do not "flatten." Without economies of scale (or institutional monopoly) free entry gives pure competition. Chamberlin's real achievement was showing that monopoly can exist without privilege, and his 1937 retreat on free entry is regrettable. Kaldor suggests a new terminology to divide the field.

## Argument in steps
1. Differences can be terminological, doctrinal, or in subject matter. Chamberlin has not shown a difference in subject matter, so his "dissimilarities" must be "misconceptions" (pp. 513–514).
2. **MR.** The "heart of the matter" is price vs MC, which needs elasticity, i.e. MR. Tangency is just Robinson's "double condition" in geometric form (pp. 514–516).
3. **Numbers.** Chamberlin confuses demand-led growth in numbers (expanding area) with supply-led changes in density. The competitive field is "n-dimensional." Most importantly, he confuses *slope with elasticity*. A leftward shift with unchanged slope still raises elasticity at equilibrium output, so price moves toward MC. Slope "is a matter of the scale of drawing" (pp. 516–519).
4. **Divisibility.** Pure competition does not need infinitely many firms. It arrives once producers take price as given. Economies of scale are a necessary (and, absent institutional monopolies, sufficient) condition for imperfect competition; Chamberlin's footnote is a "non sequitur" (pp. 520–522).
5. **Free entry and Weltanschauung.** He regrets Chamberlin's "volte-face." Showing that monopoly need not rest on privilege (the company store vs Henry Ford's scale economies) was Chamberlin's great achievement. The claimed difference in *Weltanschauung* does not exist; Robinson herself said monopoly analysis "swallowed up" competitive analysis. Proposal: reserve "monopoly" for institutional privilege; "imperfect competition" for limitation due to economies of scale with free entry; "monopolistic competition" for limitation due to restriction of entry (pp. 522–529).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Degree of market imperfection** — measured by the elasticity of the firm's demand at equilibrium, i.e. price relative to MC.
- **Density vs size of the competitive field** — numbers within a given section vs the breadth of the section.
- **Institutional monopoly vs economic causes** — privilege/restriction of entry vs economies of scale.
- **Don't confuse:** slope (depends on drawing scale) vs elasticity (does not).
