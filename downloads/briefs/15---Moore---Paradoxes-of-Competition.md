## Main claim
"Perfect competition" quietly combines five hypotheses:
- **I.** Every factor seeks maximum net income.
- **II.** There is one price in one market.
- **III.** One producer's output has a negligible effect on price.
- **IV.** One producer's output is negligible relative to total output.
- **V.** Each producer ignores the effect of his output on rivals.

It is a fallacy to carry reasoning that needs all five into industries with few producers, where III and IV fail. Cournot's duopoly and complementary-monopoly paradoxes show the problem. So the claim that labour gets what it produces holds at best under perfect competition. With few producers one can only say labour "at least produces what it gets". Moore wants statistics on how much of industry is in each condition.

## Argument in steps
1. **The five hypotheses (pp. 211–215).** Economic terms pass through stages of definition. Moore lists the five hypotheses hidden in "perfect competition".
2. **Two springs (pp. 216–219).**
   - Cournot uses I, II and V: price lands between monopoly and competition, with stable equilibrium.
   - Bertrand uses I and II, and denies III and IV: no limit to the fall in price, and no equilibrium.
   Cournot's mistake is not a mathematical slip; his conclusions follow from his premises. The trouble is that premise V is illegitimate once III and IV fail. Bertrand is "nearer the truth".
3. **Adding costs (pp. 220–224).** Edgeworth's example with limited capacity: price is undercut down to OQ, then output is restricted, and so on. There is "no resting-place", so the outcome is indeterminate and wages are set by non-competitive causes.
4. **Paradoxes from Cournot's own equations (pp. 224–227).**
   - The bigger producer has the *lower* marginal cost.
   - Under increasing returns, perfect competition has no equilibrium, yet duopoly has a stable one with price above marginal cost.
   - Marshall's notes show that "marginal product of labour" changes meaning with the number of producers.
5. **Complementary monopoly and summary (pp. 227–230).** In Cournot's ch. IX, separately monopolised factors give a higher price and lower output than a single monopoly. Moore calls these conclusions "extraordinary"; Edgeworth finds the case indeterminate. Moore ends with four limits on "the labourer gets what he produces", each with "no statistics".

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Maximum hypothesis (I)**: the essential meaning of competition.
- **Law of indifference (II)**: Jevons's one-price rule.
- **Competing monopolists**: Edgeworth's phrase for a few sellers of the same good.
- **Indeterminate**: no single stable price and quantity.
- **Don't confuse:**
  - Moore does *not* back Bertrand's charge that Cournot blundered mathematically. The fault is the premise (V).
  - "Competition" in its essential sense (I alone) is not the same as *perfect* competition (I–V).
