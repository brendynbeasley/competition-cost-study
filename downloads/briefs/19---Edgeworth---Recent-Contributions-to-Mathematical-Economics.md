## Main claim
Mathematical economics, pure or applied, yields *presumptions* ("antecedent probabilities") that are useful in practice "until cause to the contrary be shown". Edgeworth illustrates this from Pigou's *Wealth and Welfare*.

On railway rates he draws a distinction between *things*, not definitions:
- **Joint production proper** (Mill's gas and coke): the cost of x gas is the same as the cost of x gas plus y coke.
- **Cost depending on a sum** of outputs, x + y (tons of coal plus tons of copper). This is the opposite extreme, a limiting case of *rival* production.

In the second case the prime cost of carrying a ton of coal equals that of a ton of copper, so competitive rates tend to be equal. The "cost of service" rule is therefore a regulative ideal. Edgeworth sides with Pigou.

## Argument in steps
1. **Presumptions (pp. 189–191).** Pure and applied theory are "nearly equally false" if taken too literally, but both point to tendencies. Example: a tax will reduce demand for what is taxed.
2. **Two principles from Pigou (pp. 191–195).** Edgeworth defends them against the *Quarterly Review*:
   - equalising marginal net products (the "Smith-Pigou principle");
   - an elasticity of demand for labour much greater than one.
3. **Railway rates (pp. 196–197).** Pigou erred in calling the experts' mistake "an accident of language". The real issue is a difference between things: the gas-coke case versus the case where cost depends on z = x + y. Edgeworth compares symmetallism with bimetallism. The experts' "subtlety of thought" did not match the distinctions in reality.
4. **A spectrum (pp. 197–200).** Joint production proper (gas and coke), then partial jointness (wool and mutton), then independence, then rival production. Cost of the form F(ax + by) sits at the far end. Fanno rejects the "broad" concept of joint cost as "too vague and indeterminate", writing in the light of the 1913 *QJE* controversy.
5. **Result (pp. 200–202).**
   - The regulating cost is *prime* cost, which is the same per ton for coal and copper, so prices tend to be equal.
   - Monopoly obstructs this tendency, which is why it works as a rule for regulation, against the "heresy" of charging different prices for like services.
   - Edgeworth agrees with Pigou: discrimination ("value of service") fits an early stage of development, and "cost of service" should rule in developed countries, as the ICC is now enforcing.
   - The "competition" that defenders of discrimination appeal to (Hugo Meyer) is really duopoly.

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Joint production proper**: fixed proportions; one outlay produces both goods (gas and coke).
- **Rival (competitive) production**: more of one good makes the other harder to produce. Cost F(x + y) is its limiting case.
- **Prime cost**: the cost of adding a unit of one product, other things equal. Here it regulates competitive price.
- **Antecedent probability / a priori presumption**: a rule that holds *prima facie* until shown otherwise.
- **Don't confuse:** Edgeworth does not claim actual rates equal prime cost. Monopoly obstructs the tendency, and its use is as a *regulative rule*.
