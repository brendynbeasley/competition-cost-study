# 58 · Kaldor (1935) — Market Imperfection and Excess Capacity
*Economica, n.s. 2(5), pp. 33–50* · Group: E — Imperfect vs monopolistic competition · Tier: 1

**Hook (memorize this line):** Free entry into differentiated markets is "the creator of excess capacity", but only under Chamberlin's heroic assumptions.

**Responds to:** Chamberlin, Theory of Monopolistic Competition 1933, ch. V (context); Robinson, Economics of Imperfect Competition, ch. 7 (context); Shove, "The Imperfection of the Market," EJ March 1933 (cf. #47); his own #49 and #53  
**Answered by:** #63 Chamberlin 1937. He rejects Kaldor's numbers and divisibility arguments (pp. 563–565) but *accepts* that full free entry means anyone could produce an identical product, so free entry is "incompatible with a differentiated product" (pp. 566–567). Then #66 Kaldor 1938 rejoins.

## Main claim
Chamberlin's excess-capacity theorem holds: with an imperfect market and free entry, entry pushes each firm's demand curve to tangency with its falling cost curve. But the result depends on unrealistic assumptions: that preferences are evenly spread, so rivals are all equally close; that producers are short-sighted; and that institutional monopoly is absent. Drop those assumptions and tangency need not follow, although some rise in costs instead of fall in price remains likely. Robinson's version is "merely a statement of a tautology," because she counts unremoved profits as cost.

## Argument in steps
1. **The doctrine.** Many producers of "slightly different" products. Preferences evenly spread, so one firm's moves have a negligible effect on each rival. No "institutional monopoly." Economies of scale over some range. Entry continues until demand is tangent to average cost, so profits are "absorbed in lower productive efficiency" rather than passed on in lower prices (pp. 34–37).
2. **Objection 1: the scale of products.** Real products sit on a "scale" with near and far rivals (Savile Row vs East End tailors; sellers along a road). One firm's action is not spread evenly. Duopoly is "the leading species of a large genus," and the firm's demand curve is an *imagined demand curve*. Far-sighted firms that allow for potential entrants act as if demand were more elastic and may avoid excess capacity (pp. 37–41).
3. **Objection 2: divisibility.** With no economies of scale, entry continues until demand is infinitely elastic, so perfect competition establishes itself; the mathematical economists "weren't such fools after all." With economies of scale, an entrant needs a minimum output that can turn everyone's profits into losses. Indivisibility then shields profits and gives a "monopolistic advantage" to whoever is first in the field. Tangency is not necessary (pp. 41–43).
4. **Identical curves and institutional monopoly.** Identical demand curves are not essential. Identical cost curves follow from the absence of institutional monopoly (patents, trade-marks, names, unique resources, buyers' inertia). Where institutional monopoly exists it can block excess capacity or, by raising imperfection, encourage it (pp. 43–45).
5. **Summing up.** Excess capacity depends on producers' short-sightedness, on institutional monopoly, and on how far the market is a "chain relationship." Multi-product firms only change the form of waste, to "technical wastage." On welfare: standardisation or cartels could raise the money value of the national dividend, but consumers are never offered the choice between variety and cheapness (pp. 46–50).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Excess capacity** — long-run equilibrium on the falling part of the long-run cost curve (p. 34 n.).
- **Pseudo-monopolist** — a seller of a differentiated product whose cross-elasticities of demand with rivals are large but finite.
- **Imagined demand curve** — the producer's subjective view of his demand, based on expected rival reactions. It contrasts with the objective "real" curve.
- **Institutional monopoly** — anything (patent, trade-mark, name, unique resource, buyers' inertia) that stops a rival producing the identical product at the same relative cost.
- **Don't confuse:** Kaldor does *not* deny the logic ("the theory is unassailable" given its assumptions). He attacks the assumptions. Also, Robinson's tangency is tautological, Chamberlin's is substantive.
