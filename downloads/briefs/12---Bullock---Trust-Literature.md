## Main claim
The recent literature mixes up two things: large-scale production (good, and already achieved by big independent firms) and monopoly (one consolidated company replacing those firms). Trusts are monopolistic in purpose and in effect. Their claimed economies over *large* independents are slight, and the losses from monopoly outweigh them. The "ruinous competition" they cure is largely caused by tariffs and tax laws. Writers who believe in trust superiority cannot also count on potential competition to protect the public.

## Argument in steps
1. Survey (Part I, pp. 167–181). Trust literature falls into eight classes, from official reports to legal writing. One key group treats trusts as inevitable (Jenks, Collier, von Halle, Baker, Hadley); another wants competition restored (de Rousiers, Ely, Nettleton, Clark).
2. Trusts are monopolies (pp. 181–185). Combinations controlling 65 to 95 per cent of output, and data showing a wider margin between materials cost and price after combination, establish "monopolistic intent and monopolistic power" (p. 185).
3. The real issue (pp. 186–187) is not small vs large production but large independent firms vs one centralized combination. "Grasp firmly the distinction between large-scale production and monopoly."
4. The three pro-trust arguments fail (pp. 190–210). (a) Efficiency: savings on cross-freights, patents, idle plant and purchasing are small or man-made, and efficiency has a limit size beyond which monopoly breeds apathy. (b) Mass of capital to crush rivals: gives only temporary monopoly, and banning price discrimination removes it. (c) Ruinous competition: the over-investment behind it comes mainly from tariffs and tax laws (distilling, sugar, iron).
5. Potential competition cannot rescue the pro-trust view (pp. 210–214). If an independent's lowest viable price is $1 and the trust's cost is 80 cents, the trust can charge 99.9 cents and keep almost all the saving. Conclusions (pp. 214–217): stay sceptical of combination economies; permanent monopoly cannot coexist with real competition. The real causes of trusts are natural resources, railway discrimination, patents, tariffs and promoters' profits. The remedy is moderation and "live and let live."

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Large-scale production vs monopoly**: big independent plants vs centralized control of a whole industry. Bullock says the trust debate is about the latter.
- **Potential competition**: the threat of entry, which pro-trust writers say restrains trusts. Bullock: it can only stop price reaching the independents' cost.
- **Destructive competition**: price-cutting below cost under heavy fixed capital. Bullock blames tariffs and tax laws more than competition itself.
- **Don't confuse:** Bullock does *not* reject Clark's potential-competition argument. He calls it consistent (p. 211) because Clark does not think monopoly more efficient. His attack is on writers who claim both superior efficiency *and* a check from potential competition.

## Diagram

