# 07 · Adams (1887) — Relation of the State to Industrial Action
*Publications of the American Economic Association, 1(6), Jan 1887, pp. 7–85 (the volume's running numbers, in brackets on each page, are [471]–[549])* · Group: A — Trusts, monopoly & the state · Tier: 3

**Hook (memorize this line):** Adams: competition handles constant- and diminishing-returns industries; increasing returns create natural monopoly, which needs state control.

**Responds to:** Spencer, *The Man versus the State* (laissez-faire); Mill and Cairnes (laissez-faire as a mere maxim); Bastiat; the German presumption in favour of the state. Cites J. B. Clark's *Philosophy of Wealth* on business "moral dualism" (pp. 40–41)  
**Answered by:** #04 Hadley 1886 already borrows Adams's "law of the increasing return" (Hadley p. 40) to extend the railway argument to factories. #08 Marshall 1890 likewise wants public *control* of "indivisible" industries, but opposes state *management*

## Main claim
Laissez-faire is only a conservative maxim, not a scientific principle. Industrial law should keep the benefits of competition while guarding against its evils. Unregulated competition has three evils: (1) the worst competitor sets the moral level of a trade; (2) society loses the benefits of monopoly; (3) government is left weak and corrupt. Adams's famous test for evil (2): sort industries by returns to added capital and labour. Industries with increasing returns are natural monopolies, and state control "should be co-extensive with the application of the law of increasing returns" (p. 60).

## Argument in steps
1. Neither the English presumption against the state nor the German presumption for it is a principle. Society is the organism, and both state and individual are its functions (pp. 23–31).
2. Unregulated competition has three evils (p. 38). Against the first, the state can **raise the plane of competition**, for example with factory laws that encode what the decent majority already wants (pp. 39–44).
3. **Three classes of industry** (p. 55):
   - *constant returns*: 2x gives 2y, 3x gives 3y. Retail trade is the example: easy to enter, so competition regulates it.
   - *diminishing returns*: 3x gives only 2¾y. Farming is the example; "There is no call for government farming" (p. 57).
   - *increasing returns*: 3x gives more than 3y (pp. 55–59).
4. Under increasing returns an established firm can expand more cheaply than a newcomer can enter, so "free competition is powerless" to regulate it (p. 60). Railways are the case: widening the old road is cheaper than building a new one. New parallel lines are built to be sold to the old road, not to compete (pp. 61–63).
5. In such industries "no law can make them compete" (p. 64). The only choice is between an irresponsible extra-legal monopoly and a legal monopoly run on the rule of public financiering, where price equals cost (pp. 50, 64). Adams calls this a *conservative* extension of personal responsibility, not socialism (pp. 76–85).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Laws of constant / diminishing / increasing returns**: defined by the increment of product per increment of capital and labour in an expanding enterprise.
- **Industrial monopoly**: "a business superior to the regulating control of competition" (p. 48).
- **Private vs public financiering**: private firms charge the highest price that does not curtail profitable business; the state charges price = cost, guaranteed by publicity of accounts (pp. 50–51).
- **Plane of competition**: the legal floor (no child labour, normal working day, and so on) that shapes competition without restricting it.
- **Don't confuse:** (a) Adams's increasing returns (cheap expansion by one established firm, which means natural monopoly) with Marshall's later industry-level increasing returns (context). (b) High profits from *superior management*, which Adams calls a rental of business talent and which does not justify regulation (p. 56), with monopoly profit, which comes from price exceeding necessary cost.

## Diagram
Adams uses a simple algebraic table, not a figure (pp. 55–59):
```
capital+labour:   2x  ->  3x
constant:         2y  ->  3y      competition regulates (retail)
diminishing:      2y  ->  2¾y     competition regulates (farming)
increasing:       2y  ->  >3y     natural monopoly -> state control (railways)
```

## One line to quote
> "the principle of free competition is powerless to exercise a healthy regulating influence" (p. 60)
