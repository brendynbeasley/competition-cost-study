**Hook (memorize this line):** When a steel monopolist meets an ore monopolist, whoever dictates the price decides the outcome. Otherwise it is indeterminate.

**Responds to:** Wicksell's last published paper, a criticism of Bowley's *Mathematical Groundwork of Economics* (p. 62 on bilateral monopoly), reprinted by Schumpeter in *Archiv für Sozialwissenschaft*, Bd. 58

## Main claim
In bilateral monopoly, a monopolist manufacturer buys from a monopolist supplier of a material, one unit of material per unit of product. The result depends on who sets the price:
- **Case I.** The manufacturer dictates the price, and the supplier's offer schedule constrains him.
- **Case II.** The supplier dictates the price, and the manufacturer's demand schedule constrains him. This is Wicksell's case.
- **Case III.** The two combine. Output and joint gain are at their maximum, where the two schedules intersect, but that price is *not* stable.

Wicksell's solution is one-sided. Without collusion or an arbitrator there is no definite solution. Bowley applies this to a trade union facing coal-owners.

## Argument in steps
1. **Setup (p. 651).** Consumers' demand is p = f(x). The ore cost schedule is φ(x).
   - Steel-maker maximises {f(x) − π}x.
   - Mine-owner maximises {π − φ(x)}x.
   Bowley had called the problem indeterminate. Wicksell objected, solving only the case where the supplier sets the price and costs are zero.
2. **Geometry (pp. 652–654).** Demand line AC₀ and cost line BC₀. The supplier's *offer line* is BR₀; the manufacturer's *demand line for material* is AR₀.
3. **The three cases (pp. 653–656).**
   - Case I: the manufacturer's gain is largest.
   - Case II: the supplier's gain is largest.
   - Case III: output x₀ is largest and the *joint* gain is greatest. At that price each still wants to move π: the manufacturer down towards R₁, the supplier up towards R₂.
4. **Linear results (pp. 656–657).**
   - U₀ + V₀ exceeds both U₁ + V₁ and U₂ + V₂.
   - With equal slopes, x₁ = x₂ = (a − b)/6m. Joint gain is (a − b)²/9m in Case I or II, against (a − b)²/8m when combined.
   - The result extends to several materials, each monopolised.
5. **Labour monopoly (pp. 657–659).** A union that dictates the wage is Case II. In the coal example with 50,000 miners:
   - a wage of 5s. 4d. gives full employment;
   - 6s. gives maximum earnings;
   - 7s. 6d. gives maximum receipts including unemployment benefit.
   If the employer dictates (Case I), he drives the wage down. Bowley concludes there is "no definite solution", though an arbitrator could work within narrow limits.

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Bilateral monopoly**: a single seller facing a single buyer, here of an intermediate good.
- **Offer schedule**: the supplier's output response to a given price, π = φ(x) + xφ′(x).
- **Manufacturer's demand schedule**: his demand for the material, π = f(x) + xf′(x).
- **Don't confuse:** the Case III intersection price maximises joint gain but is not an equilibrium. It needs an outside arbitrator. Wicksell found only Case II.
