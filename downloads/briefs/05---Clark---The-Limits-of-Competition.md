# 05 · Clark (1887) — The Limits of Competition
*Political Science Quarterly, 2(1), Mar 1887, pp. 45–61* · Group: A — Trusts, monopoly & the state · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** Clark: competition's war ends in "federations of giants"; accept, study and regulate combinations, and rely on residual competition.

**Responds to:** Ricardo's system, which Clark reads as an "economic Darwinism" of a transitional era; Cairnes's "non-competing groups" (quoted at length, pp. 48–50). Reaches the same accept-and-regulate view as #04 Hadley 1886  
**Answered by:** #06 Giddings, the very next article in the same PSQ issue. Giddings builds on Clark's grouping by stage of production and supplies the "practical observation" of residual competition that Clark hands off at p. 58. #12 Bullock 1901 discusses Clark's *later* papers, in which potential competition restrains trusts

## Main claim
Ricardian competition belonged to a passing age of struggle between unequal rivals. In manufacturing and transport the survivors are now few, strong and nearly equal, so they combine. Producers' combinations are a normal product of evolution. They should be "accepted, studied, and, probably, regulated," not suppressed. Prices in these industries are then held in check by competition in "latent and residual" forms.

## Argument in steps
1. Ricardo built permanent laws on a transitional "struggle for existence" between unequal producers (pp. 45–46). That contest is now nearly over: "armed neutralities and federations of giants" (p. 47).
2. Cairnes's non-competing groups rested on *potential* competition, i.e. children free to enter better-paid trades. Machinery and education have since erased those class lines; only unions might rebuild them artificially (pp. 48–54).
3. The real barriers now come from employers' combinations in glass, wall-paper, cut nails, anthracite, steel rails and more. Suppressing them would mean "social retrogradation" (pp. 54–55).
4. Competition really runs along *horizontal* strata of production (wood-cutting, lumber-dealing, transport, cabinet-making, finishing). The groups that compete are the groups that combine. Fixing prices limits actual competition; restricting output limits potential competition, i.e. the flow of capital and labour between strata (pp. 56–58).
5. Combination shifts the war so it is fought "across the horizontal lines" (p. 58), between strata. Residual competition survives inside the pool and between the pool and outside capital. Combination needs few, equal rivals and a portable product (silk yes, baskets no; agriculture has too many producers), and it is helped by cheap freight and tariffs. Conclusion: regulate, don't suppress (pp. 58–61).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Predatory competition / economic Darwinism**: the transitional struggle between unequal rivals that Ricardo generalized from.
- **Actual vs potential competition**: underselling a rival in your own line vs the possibility that labour or capital moves in from other lines (Cairnes's sense).
- **Residual competition**: *actual* competition among pool members of roughly equal strength, plus *potential* competition between the whole combination and the rest of society (p. 58).
- **Horizontal (functional) groups**: strata of the production chain. True competition, and therefore combination, happens within a stratum.
- **Don't confuse:** Clark's groups (by stage of production, real competition) with Cairnes's groups (by personal or class qualification, potential competition). Giddings then adds a *third* grouping, by mental capacity, which cuts across Clark's.

## Diagram
Clark's furniture-making group (p. 56). The strata are stacked from raw material at the bottom to finished product at the top. Competition and combination run *within* each layer; after combination, conflict runs *between* layers.

  Cabinet making
  Transporting
  Lumber dealing     (each layer: capitalists + workmen)
  Wood cutting       <- raw-material stage
  pre-combination war: inside a layer  <-->

## Likely exam angle
- Clark 1887 vs Giddings 1887 (same issue). Clark says competition is being limited and replaced by combination plus residual competition. Giddings says competition persists in new forms and pools cannot hold.
- Potential competition as a check on combinations. Bullock #12 later attacks this idea when it is used by trust enthusiasts, but he exempts Clark's later version.
