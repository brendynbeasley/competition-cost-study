# 51 · Robinson (1941) — Rising Supply Price
*Economica, New Series 8(29), Feb 1941, pp. 1–8* · Group: D2 — The 1930 symposium & decreasing costs · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** Robinson: when demand *shifts* to a commodity, it turns relative factor prices against itself, so supply price rises.

**Responds to:** Marshall's supply curve; Hicks, *Value and Capital* (context, not in your folder); Sraffa 1926 (context); #33 Pigou 1927; #48 Robbins 1934 (p. 5 n.); her own *Economics of Imperfect Competition* (1933, context)

## Main claim
Robinson calls her purpose "frankly escapist". The proper value-theory question is how supply responds to a *transfer* of demand, not to a net increase (Marshall's "queer problem"). Assume full employment, perfect competition, no economies of large-scale industry and, first, fixed factor supplies. Then any industry that uses factors in other than average proportions has rising supply price, measured in a composite unit of resources. Its strength depends on size, idiosyncrasy, low elasticity of substitution and inelastic factor supply. Falling supply price can occur only when factor supplies vary.

## Argument in steps
1. Three separate questions get confused: Ricardian output-as-a-whole, short-period employment, and the value-theory question of relative prices (p. 1).
2. Marshall answers a net increase in demand, which with full employment presupposes more resources: "putting the cart before the horse". Hicks relies on a fixed number of firms and an "optical illusion" from his choice of numéraire (pp. 2–3).
3. Demand moves to "alpha" from commodities in general. If alpha uses factors in average proportions, supply price is constant. If not, the prices of its favoured factors rise in terms of the composite unit, so it has rising supply price (pp. 4–5).
4. Strength: (1) alpha's share of all factors, (2) how idiosyncratic its factor needs are, (3) low elasticity of substitution, (4) once factor supplies vary, inelastic supply of the factors it needs (pp. 5–7).
5. With variable factor supplies, falling supply price can occur: Pigou's "decreasing supply price simpliciter". Marshall's agriculture/manufacture contrast is justified, but the real line is rare vs common, "cranky vs adaptable", inelastic vs elastic factors. Manufactures: near-constant supply price (pp. 7–8).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Transfer vs net increase of demand** — the right question vs Marshall's question.
- **Composite unit of resources** — factors weighted by their proportions in industry as a whole; used to measure supply price.
- **Don't confuse:** this is *rising* supply price under *perfect* competition from factor prices, not Robinson 1932's falling supply price under imperfect competition. Rising supply price needs no change in factor proportions within alpha (p. 5 n.).
