**Responds to:** Marshall, *Principles* Bk IV ch. xiii and Bk V (context, not in your folder); Robertson's 1927 Colwyn Report article (context); H. D. Henderson, *Supply and Demand* (context); notes #35 Pigou's "equilibrium firm" (written before it, fn.)

## Main claim
Marshall's representative firm, an afterthought from the 2nd edition, is not needed for any problem in value theory. Unequal managers earn unequal normal profits, just as unequal lands earn unequal rents. Worse, the concept is misleading: it hides the variety of managerial ability, suggests profits tend to equality, and suggests there is one optimum firm size.

## Argument in steps
1. Marshall's definition: a firm with "a fairly long life and fair success", normal ability, and normal access to economies. It is a long-period business unit, not a plant and not a statistical average. So statistics can neither refute it nor make it useful (pp. 387–391).
2. Per Robertson, it was designed for cases where producers differ in efficiency. Robbins separates **static** disparities (scarce best factors) from **dynamic** ones (maladjustment during change) (pp. 391–392).
3. Static equilibrium needs no representative firm. Each factor must earn at least what it could get elsewhere. Unequal profits are rent-like, and extra-marginal entrepreneurs become workers or officials. The same holds for Marshall's "stationariness" with its "trees of a virgin forest" and for dynamic change (pp. 392–397).
4. Partial equilibrium and diminishing cost: output can change through the *number* of firms, so the representative firm's cost curve may stop being representative. External economies are doubtful (citing Young and Robertson) and are partly a matter of management (pp. 397–399).
5. Misleading: Henderson's claim that the marginal concern is run by a manager of average ability ignores diminishing returns to scarce talent. "If all entrepreneurs were at least of average managerial ability, they would at once cease to be average." Footnote: Young's point that growth by differentiation dissolves the firm. Conclusions: no tendency of profits to equality; many optimum sizes; the Colwyn debate needs no representative firm (pp. 399–404).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Representative firm (Marshall)** — a typical long-lived business with normal ability and normal access to internal and external economies for a given total output.
- **Static vs dynamic disparities** — efficiency differences that would last in equilibrium versus those caused by change.
- **Unequal normal profits** — like differential rents, a reward for scarce ability.
- **Don't confuse:** Robbins's target is *Marshall's* representative firm. He wrote before seeing Pigou's "equilibrium firm" (#35) and only hopes his remarks apply to it (fn., p. 387).
