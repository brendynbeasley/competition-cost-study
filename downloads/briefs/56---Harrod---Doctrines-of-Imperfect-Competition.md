# 56 · Harrod (1934) — Doctrines of Imperfect Competition
*Quarterly Journal of Economics, 48(3), pp. 442–470* · Group: E — Imperfect vs monopolistic competition · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** Downward-sloping demand means either excess profits at optimum scale, or normal profits with too-small, under-used plant.

**Responds to:** Robinson and Chamberlin's 1933 books (context); his own EJ 1930–1933 notes (#41, #43, #45); Viner 1931 on the envelope (context); Pigou, Industrial Fluctuations (context)  
**Answered by:** #57 Robinson 1934 (same definition of perfect competition, but Harrod slips in free entry, p. 106; she disputes his link between the two-level profit gap and imperfect competition, p. 108). Continued by Harrod himself in #61 (1936)

## Main claim
Observation shows that most sources of supply outside organised exchanges face downward-sloping demand. In imperfect competition, long-period equilibrium is consistent with lay-outs below optimum size worked below optimum capacity (if only standard profits are earned) or with excess profits (if the optimum is reached). Price is above the competitive level either way. This distorts resource allocation as much as tariffs do, makes "a large breach in the defence of laissez-faire," and allows decreasing costs, which destabilises the general level of output in the trade cycle.

## Argument in steps
1. Under perfect competition a source can never be in a range of decreasing costs, and its plant tends to optimum size. Every producer of non-homogeneous goods is "something of a monopolist"; in equilibrium, elasticity of demand must be > 1 (pp. 442–446).
2. Technical apparatus: MR = d(xy)/dx; long-period cost is the *envelope* of short-period curves, not the locus of their minima; a "standard rate of profit" (pp. 446–455).
3. Imperfect competition: if the demand curve happens to allow optimum lay-out, excess profits persist. Newcomers are excluded because they must make substantial price concessions. If entry is easy and only standard profits are earned, demand is tangent to the falling long-run cost curve, and the lay-out is sub-optimal and under-used (pp. 456–460). In factor markets, marginal outlay exceeds the factor's price; trade unions make the labour market "more perfect" (pp. 460–461).
4. Optimum: resources yield equi-marginal *revenue*, not equi-marginal *utility*. An elasticity of 3 distorts like a 50% tariff, and an elasticity of 6 like a 20% tariff. There is a case for public wage-fixing and cautious compulsory "rationalisation." Marketing expenses have the same distorting effects (pp. 461–464).
5. Trade cycle: with decreasing-cost industries, equilibrium of total output is unstable. Pigou's reciprocal-demand argument assumes increasing costs. The psychological theory works without persistent error (pp. 465–470).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Standard rate of profit** — the lowest rate that induces the firm to invest in fixed equipment.
- **Lay-out / source of supply** — material plant plus immaterial fixed aids (experience, connections). A source can be a plant or a firm.
- **Marginal outlay** — the extra expenditure to obtain one more unit of a factor under a rising supply curve.
- **Don't confuse:** Harrod's two imperfect-competition equilibria (optimum lay-out + excess profit vs tangency + sub-optimal lay-out).
