# 08 · Marshall (1890) — "Some Aspects of Competition" (Presidential Address, Section F, British Association, Leeds, Sept 1890)
*Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, 53(4), Dec 1890, pp. 612–643* · Group: A — Trusts, monopoly & the state · Tier: 1

**Hook (memorize this line):** Marshall: competition vs combination is a "false antithesis"; publicly control indivisible industries, but don't let the state run them.

**Responds to:** The older English economists' dogmatism, especially on Protection in new countries (List, Carey, Mill's infant-industry concession; Patten is rejected); the American trust movement and a Congressional committee's view that combination grows naturally out of competition; Senator Sherman's bill, passed by the Senate on 8 April 1890; German-style state socialism and "a few of the younger American economists" under German influence (p. 629; he names no one). Credits Baker, *Monopolies and the People*, and mentions Rae and Foxwell (p. 630n)  
**Answered by:** #12 Bullock 1901, who quotes Marshall's point that a monopoly must keep "bargaining and manoeuvring on a large scale" and also cites his remarks on central responsibility vs individual energy and on English vs American corporations

## Main claim
Economists must not repeat with trusts the mistake they made with Protection, condemning wholesale what is partly valid. Combinations and partial monopolies will be permanent features, with good and bad effects, so the job is analysis, not dogma. Law that bans loose combinations while letting a single firm do the same things rests on a "false antithesis" between competition and combination. For indivisible industries (gas, water, railways), Marshall wants tight public *control*, possibly public *ownership*, but private *management*. In ordinary industries, the many independent firms bring an inventive energy that big combinations lack. Public opinion is a growing economic force that can curb abuses.

## Argument in steps
1. **Method lesson from Protection** (pp. 613–621). The old economists' creed-like doctrines ignored the new-country arguments: infant industries, and the fact that the Law of Increasing Return in manufacture favours protecting it. By "killing the messenger" they lost credibility. Hostility to trusts must not repeat that error.
2. **The trust movement** (pp. 621–625). England's Free Trade has kept increasing return from strengthening combinations there as much as in protectionist America. Pressure from combinations is becoming "more extensive, but less intensive" (p. 622). The American complaint: firms with big plants will sell at Prime Cost in slack times, so "competition burns so furiously as to smother itself in its own smoke" (p. 623). Marshall's reply: the movement is exaggerated, and Standard Oil has special natural and transport advantages. Smart trusts now follow a *moderate* price policy, since high prices bring cheating, entry and public anger.
3. **Pooling slides into consolidation** (pp. 625–628). A pool weakens each member's incentive to manage well, so it is "on an inclined plane" toward full amalgamation. The law encourages this by banning pools and loose combinations while allowing a single consolidated firm. The Sherman bill is interesting because it extends criticism to *individuals* who monopolize.
4. **Indivisible industries** (pp. 628–631). Here public interference "must be absolute" (p. 629), but Anglo-Saxon economists prefer private management because it is more inventive and better at spotting talent, while state departments invite political jobbery. His idea: the public owns the franchise and leases it, with firms *competing for the franchise* on the price or quality they offer.
5. **Large combinations in competitive trades** (pp. 631–634). They save waste in bargaining, but must bargain to defend their monopoly. Their claim to stabilize industry is unproven. Many industries (textiles) reach maximum economy at moderate size. Trusts exploit *existing* knowledge, while a multitude of independent undertakers produces more *new* knowledge. Conclusion (pp. 641–643): extend public control and public opinion to escape both "irresponsible competition" and "ironbound socialism."

## Key terms & distinctions
- **False antithesis**: treating combination as the opposite of competition. Marshall's examples: unions were condemned while the employer, himself a combination of capital, was not; pools are banned while amalgamation is allowed.
- **Indivisible industries**: Marshall's preferred name for "monopolies" such as gas, water, railways, tramways and electricity, where only one firm can sensibly serve an area.
- **State control vs state management**: he supports the first (and sometimes ownership), and opposes the second for England and America.
- **Competition in bargaining vs competition in production**: bargaining is "almost pure waste" (p. 631); productive and marketing competition serve the public.
- **Prime Cost**: the actual outlay for an extra unit, the floor to which firms with large plants cut prices in a slump (p. 623).
- **Extensive vs intensive pressure**: better transport lets combinations cover wider areas but lessens their grip at any one point.
- **Don't confuse:** Marshall is not pro-trust and not for laissez-faire. He is sceptical of trusts' claimed economies and of state management alike, and he relies on control, franchise competition and public opinion.
