**Hook (memorize this line):** Young: the division of labour depends on the division of labour, so increasing returns make growth cumulative.

**Responds to:** Marshall's internal/external economies and representative firm; Adam Smith (division of labour limited by the market); implicitly Sraffa 1926 (context, not in your folder) and the supply-curve apparatus of #33/#35  
**Answered by:** 1930 symposium #38 (group D2); compare #34 Robbins (who credits Young's idea in a footnote)

## Main claim
Increasing returns cannot be seen by studying one firm or one industry. They come from the progressive division of labour among industries and from more roundabout (capitalistic) methods, both limited by the size of the market. Since the market is itself total production, "the division of labour depends in large part upon the division of labour". Change therefore spreads cumulatively and does not settle into equilibrium. The supply-and-demand apparatus gets in the way of seeing this.

## Argument in steps
1. Presidential address, Section F, British Association, Glasgow, 10 Sept 1928. Marshall's internal/external split guards against "increasing returns means monopoly", but it is a partial view. External economies are not just the sum of firms' internal economies; that field changes in kind as well as size (pp. 527–528).
2. Take Adam Smith's theorem as the text. The key economies are those of roundabout methods: a hammer for one nail is wasteful; Ford's methods only pay at huge output. There are also secondary economies in making the machines (pp. 529–530).
3. Two points: the economies are those of capitalistic methods, not of large firms as such; and they depend on the market, so "rationalisation" cannot just seize them. America's productivity reflects its large market, not better managers (pp. 531–532).
4. The market is the volume of production. With elastic reciprocal demand, more of one good is more demand for others, so growth spreads cumulatively. This is a moving equilibrium; human resistance and capital accumulation make it take time (pp. 533–535).
5. Industrial *differentiation* (printing gives rise to pulp, ink, type and machinery makers). "The representative firm… loses its identity." Three conclusions: see industry as an interrelated whole; increasing returns come through the division of labour and roundabout methods; market and division of labour depend on each other (pp. 537–540). Note: indifference-curve construction (pp. 540–542).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Roundabout (capitalistic) methods** — using labour indirectly through tools and machines; worthwhile only at large output.
- **Division of labour among industries / industrial differentiation** — new specialised industries split off as the market grows; the main vehicle of increasing returns.
- **Large production vs large-scale production** — increasing returns come from the size of total output, not the size of firms.
- **Don't confuse:** Young 1928 (macro, cumulative growth; anti-equilibrium) vs Young 1913 #26 (micro critique of Pigou's rent-as-cost). Young does *not* say increasing returns lead to monopoly; he rejects that.

## Diagram
Mathematical note (pp. 540–542): a collective indifference curve I; equal-cost curves d (concave, decreasing returns), c (straight, constant), i (increasing returns between P and P₁). Moving along i needs extra, indirect costs that depend on the rate of movement, like a "mountain climber" setting his pace. Not central to the argument.
