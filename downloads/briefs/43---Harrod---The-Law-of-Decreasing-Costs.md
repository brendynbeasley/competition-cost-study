## Main claim
Decreasing costs are compatible with competitive equilibrium if there are competitive marketing costs, or if the firm faces a falling demand curve, or both. With marketing costs, supply price depends on the *state of demand* as well as on output, so the usual two-dimensional supply curve breaks down. Decreasing costs can be *normal* to industries where the optimum plant size grows faster than demand. So "increasing returns industries" is a legitimate idea.

## Argument in steps
1. **Section I:** marginal competitive marketing costs are the cost of trespassing into a neighbour's territory. If extra demand is spread evenly, a firm can expand without trespass. So cost depends on output *and* the state of demand: "every demand schedule has its own appropriate supply schedule", and a three-dimensional picture is needed (pp. 567–568). The supply price after a rise in demand can be lower: decreasing costs in the ordinary sense (p. 568).
2. **Section II:** profit is possible with falling marginal productive cost. When the firm's demand curve slopes down, the marginal increment of receipts falls short of price by price/elasticity. Harrod says MC = price − price/elasticity "has universal applicability", and perfect competition is the case of infinite elasticity (p. 570). Fig. 1 shows normal profit with falling costs (pp. 571–572).
3. **Section III, long period:** won't firms below optimum size merge? Not if transport costs rise with concentration. Nor if "individualism", firms' wish to keep their identity, persists (pp. 572–573).
4. **Section IV:** decreasing costs are normal where technical progress makes the optimum plant grow faster than demand. Even then a firm builds the plant that meets expected normal demand most cheaply. Fig. 2: a family of cost parabolas and their **envelope** (the "long-period productive cost curve"). Where the envelope falls, the chosen plant is worked below its own optimum (pp. 574–576).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Marginal competitive marketing costs** — the cost of holding or pushing the frontier of a firm's "sphere of influence".
- **Short-/long-period decreasing costs** — a short-/long-period rise in demand lowers marginal cost (p. 566 n.).
- **Long-period productive cost curve** — the envelope of the parabolas; tangent to each at the output that plant serves best.
- **Don't confuse:** Harrod says decreasing costs *may* occur. Robinson 1932 says equilibrium average costs *must* fall, and objects that Harrod treats the tangency as an accident (p. 548 n.).
