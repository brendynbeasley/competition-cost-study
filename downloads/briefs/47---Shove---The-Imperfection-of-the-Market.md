# 47 · Shove (1933), with reply by Robinson — The Imperfection of the Market: A Further Note
*Economic Journal, 43(169), Mar 1933, pp. 113–125* · Group: D2 — The 1930 symposium & decreasing costs · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** Shove asks Robinson: what does your cost curve show, and what is "normal" profit when entry is costly?

**Responds to:** #44 Robinson 1932; defends his own #38 (1930, pp. 106, 108)
**Answered by:** Robinson's reply in the same piece (pp. 124–125); #45 Harrod 1933 (builds on Shove's normal-profit point, citing p. 119)

## Main claim
Shove does not reject Robinson's conclusion. He questions her proof. (1) The cost of a given output depends on *how* the firm expands, and that depends on the character of demand. So her AC curve cannot stay fixed while demand shifts. (2) With costs of entry, "normal profit" is a range. Kahn's tangency then shows only maximum profit, not a necessary condition for an industry with no tendency to change output. His conclusion: cost both affects and is affected by demand, and demand is "created and moulded" by producers.

## Argument in steps
1. A small matter: he never held that falling firm costs imply falling supply price. In 1930 he said enlarging existing firms is "often (though not always or necessarily)" the method used (pp. 113–114).
2. **First question:** Robinson rightly says an *industry* supply curve depends on the kind of demand change. The same is true of the *firm*: why don't old firms open suburban branches? In her three examples, expansion of the relevant kind would *raise* costs (pp. 114–117).
3. Her curve cannot be minimum cost (then Kahn's theorem fails, since receipts may exceed minimum cost). It cannot be actual cost either (that moves with demand). So "what exactly are the costs to be set against each output?" (pp. 118–119).
4. **Second question:** with entry costs, an industry has no tendency to change output while profits sit between the exit level and the entry level. Robinson's equilibrium is then a definition (profit at the entry maximum), not a condition (pp. 119–121).
5. Alternative: split cost into **minimum cost** and **special cost** (advertising, transport, facilities). Three equilibrium rules. A change in demand alters costs as well as output (pp. 122–124).
- **Robinson's reply** (pp. 124–125): the two methods work on different terrain. She concedes an unstated assumption: costs vary only with output. She charges Shove with not separating *firm* equilibrium (MR = MC) from *industry* equilibrium (entry: AC vs price). She agrees with his third point.

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Minimum cost vs special cost** — the cheapest technical way to produce an output vs extra spending to raise its selling value.
- **Normal profit as a range** — between the level that triggers entry and the level that triggers exit.
- **Don't confuse:** the JSTOR title lists "Shove and Joan Robinson" because her reply is printed at the end. The note itself is Shove's. Don't confuse it with Pigou's note (#46), printed just before it in the same issue.
