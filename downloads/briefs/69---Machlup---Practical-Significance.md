## Main claim
"Practical" here means useful for applied analysis and government control, not for pure theorists or businessmen. Product differentiation among very many sellers ("polypoly") probably yields results not much different from pure competition. The common and important case is oligopoly, a *subjective* pattern of conjectures about rivals that produces price rigidity and higher selling costs. Oligopoly is hard to study empirically or theoretically. Still, the 1930s theory helps administrators by showing the problem is difficult, identifying relevant factors, warning against naive methods, and supplying concepts.

## Argument in steps
1. Four users of theory: pure theorist, applied analyst, government official, businessman. Practicality means correct plus applicable, which requires ideal-type assumptions. Businessmen have little to learn from theorists (pp. 227–229).
2. "Monopolistic competition" is not defined by the MR/MC tools (Cournot's centenary). Narrow sense: product differentiation. Wide sense: all intermediate cases, including oligopoly. What is new is the emphasis on differentiation and the frequency of oligopoly (pp. 229–231).
3. Polypoly with differentiated products is welcomed for its realism and anti-laissez-faire implications, but probably gives results close to pure competition. Markets break into small overlapping oligopolistic groups (pp. 231–232).
4. Oligopoly is defined by the seller's state of mind: he includes rivals' reactions. It is the most common case. Vague conjectures cause price rigidity and increased selling costs, which is a "bad blow for general equilibrium theory." From ten years as an executive (about 20% of national output), Machlup found neither differentiation nor oligopoly much affected decisions when demand was elastic. Sensible businessmen act as economists suppose, though they rationalise misleadingly (pp. 232–234).
5. "Theoretical case studies" can rank which assumptions matter, but oligopoly involves the behaviour of a few individuals ("Whether Mr. Firestone had had a good breakfast"). The theory nonetheless gives five kinds of aid to policy-makers (pp. 234–236).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Polypoly** — very many sellers. **Oligopoly** — a seller consciously allows for rivals' reactions. **Monopoly** — a seller believes rivals' reactions need not concern him.
- **Narrow vs wide sense** of "monopolistic competition" — product differentiation only vs all intermediate market forms.
- **Don't confuse:** Machlup's classification is subjective (based on the seller's conjectures), not based on objective numbers.
