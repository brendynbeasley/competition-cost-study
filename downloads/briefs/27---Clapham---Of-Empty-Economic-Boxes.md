# 27 · J. H. Clapham (1922) — Of Empty Economic Boxes
*Economic Journal, 32(127), Sep 1922, pp. 305–314* · Group: D1 Pigou's welfare economics & the cost controversy to 1928 · Tier: 1

**Hook (memorize this line):** Historian Clapham tours a hat factory and finds he cannot put any industry in the "returns" boxes.

**Responds to:** Pigou, *The Economics of Welfare* (1920) (context, not in your folder); Marshall, *Principles* and *Industry and Trade* (context)  
**Answered by:** #28 Pigou, "Empty Economic Boxes: A Reply" (Dec 1922); Clapham's rejoinder in the same issue's Notes and Memoranda (context, not in your folder); #31 Robertson 1924

## Main claim
The categories of diminishing, constant and increasing returns, on which Pigou builds conclusions about welfare and taxes, have never been filled with real industries. Nobody knows whether coal, boots or hats fall under diminishing or increasing returns. Building policy conclusions on boxes that "cannot be specified" is dangerous for a practical science.

## Argument in steps
1. The parable: an economist trained in the "dominant British school" has mental boxes labelled by laws of returns (and monopolies and taxes within them), but no one has ever said "Increasing Return Industry, hats". *The Economics of Welfare* gives no illustration in nearly a thousand pages (p. 305).
2. Trying to box hats fails: what is a unit of resources, a unit of product, "an industry" (national or world)? Each raw material (coal, rabbit fur, shellac, pulp, wool for 64's Botany tops) is uncertain, part of a world market, or a joint product (pp. 306–310).
3. Constant returns "must always remain a mathematical point, their box an empty one" (p. 310).
4. Marshall's increasing returns come from *organisation* only and exclude invention (blankets passage). But you cannot separate invention from organisation (e.g. freezing water-logged strata to sink the Doncaster coal pits). Either the boxes stay empty, or you lump inventions in (pp. 310–311).
5. Answering the imagined "analytic": harm comes from not admitting the boxes are empty. Even filled, they would give little practical guidance. Marshall's tax rule depends on "other things being equal". By the time scholars finish, "the world has moved on" (pp. 312–313). If filling is worth trying at all, start with simple diminishing-return goods such as wheat, timber and rubber (p. 314).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Empty boxes** — theoretical categories (diminishing/constant/increasing return industries) that no one has filled with actual industries.
- **"Analytic" vs historian** — Clapham's mock labels for the pure theorist versus the student "not of categories but of things".
- **Organisation vs invention** — Marshall's increasing returns come from better organisation as an industry grows, not from inventions. Clapham says the two cannot be measured apart.
- **Don't confuse:** Clapham does not deny that increasing returns exist; he denies they can be measured ("only a denial of their measurability", p. 314). Robertson's "Those Empty Boxes" (#31) is a different paper that criticises Pigou's *apparatus*, not the lack of facts.
