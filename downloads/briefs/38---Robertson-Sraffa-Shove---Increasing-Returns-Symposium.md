## Main claim
The editor calls it a three-step debate: a "partial rehabilitation of Marshallian orthodoxy" (Robertson), "negative and destructive criticisms" (Sraffa), "constructive suggestions" (Shove) (p. 79). The question is how competitive equilibrium can coexist with increasing returns that come from *internal* economies.

### Robertson (pp. 80–89)
1. Part I: the Representative Firm is needed to separate normal profit from the losses of firms "on the road to elimination". Robbins says some trees are tall because they are oaks; Robertson replies that "some oaks are taller than other oaks" (p. 81).
2. Part II lists the escape routes: Schumpeter denies the falling supply curve; Sraffa turns to monopoly; Pigou relies on external economies (and the "external-internal" hybrid of 1928); Young widens external economies (pp. 84–86).
3. Pigou's equilibrium firm (Fig. 1) has price = AC = MC. Marshall's representative firm (Fig. 2) can be in equilibrium with falling AC and price > MC. The reason: no "identifiable entity" has both the power and the motive to expand. Firms are water-drops in a wave (pp. 88–89).

### Sraffa (pp. 89–93)
1. Nobody denies that internal economies drive the move *towards* equilibrium. At equilibrium they must have stopped, so the firm sits at minimum average cost.
2. Restated, Robertson's argument says the representative firm is "a position occupied by a firm" (p. 91). But if new, bigger firms are cheaper, "why didn't they come into existence before?" (p. 92).
3. Rejoinder: under free competition the firm's demand curve is horizontal, so it is *tangent* to the cost curve at its minimum. No "Providence" is needed (p. 93). Marshall's theory cannot be both self-consistent and true to the facts, so "it is Marshall's theory that should be discarded" (p. 93).
- Robertson's reply in between (pp. 92–93): diminishing utility always operates yet helps fix equilibrium, so why not internal economies too? And don't ask "why slavery was not abolished" sooner.

### Shove (pp. 94–116)
1. He sides with Robbins: the representative firm is "brilliant" but not indispensable. Equilibrium can be stated as the placing of heterogeneous units of resources ("a jig-saw puzzle rather than a problem in hydro-dynamics", p. 99).
2. He replaces internal/external with new labels: **economies of large-scale industry** (the whole industry grows), **economies of individual expansion** (one firm grows while industry output stays fixed), **economies of concentration** (must be zero in equilibrium) and **economies of rationalisation** (pp. 101–105).
3. First answer: invading a rival's market costs more than adding to your own (transport, buyers' preferences). So internal economies can give diminishing supply price while no firm takes over the trade (pp. 105–107). This matches Pigou's "marginal substitute" vs "marginal additive" cost (p. 106 n.).
4. Second answer, with a perfect market: **time**. Luck and ability change while a firm expands, so the lead passes from firm to firm (pp. 109–113). A bigger market raises the rate at which young firms can grow (p. 113, credited to E. A. G. Robinson).
5. Marshall himself limited the "trees" analogy to the era before joint-stock companies. Shove proposes "a cluster of variable stars" instead (pp. 114–115).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Representative firm** — Robertson: a type or mode with "normal" access to economies. Sraffa's reading: "a position occupied by a firm".
- **Economies of individual expansion vs of large-scale industry** — Shove's replacement for internal vs external.
- **Don't confuse:** Sraffa *here* defends the logic of perfect competition (tangency at minimum cost) to show Marshall is inconsistent. He is not defending perfect competition as realistic. Also don't confuse Pigou's "external-internal" economies (described by Robertson) with Shove's new labels.
