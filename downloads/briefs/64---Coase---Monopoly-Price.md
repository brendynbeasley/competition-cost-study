## Main claim
Robinson's monopolist equates MC and MR with full knowledge in stationary conditions. Coase examines a combine formed out of a previously perfectly competitive industry, with entry barred. Ignorance of MR/MC logic and of the demand curve, the gap between short-run and long-run demand, the effect of price rises on future demand, non-stationary conditions, and Hicks's "quiet life" all mean that price at first rises *less* than Robinson predicts. But as demand curves fall and become more elastic with progress, the monopolist in the long run restricts output *more*, and progress slows.

## Argument in steps
1. **Does the monopolist know MR = MC?** Businessmen's cost accounts make marginal cost hard to find. Trial-and-error assumes stationary conditions and a small firm. Dim awareness "would enhance the importance of the status quo." The demand curve is even harder to know than cost (pp. 17–19).
2. **Uncertainty about demand.** Only the current price point is certain. The monopolist raises price until extra net revenue is offset by the falling probability of getting it. Each new price reveals another point, so price climbs toward, but not to, the monopoly price. More precisely he faces a *demand surface* (frequency curves of output at each price), and his attitude to risk matters (pp. 19–22).
3. **Short-run vs long-run demand.** If the monopolist looks only at the less elastic short-run curve, price ends *above* the long-run monopoly price. If he knows both curves, he probably charges about the long-run price at once. Overall, price is probably "rather higher" than with the long-run curve alone (pp. 22–25). He cannot tell whether his errors in estimating demand push price up or down (pp. 25–26).
4. **Price changes shift demand.** A higher price sets consumers and inventors experimenting with substitutes: Greek currants vs raisins, the Stevenson rubber restriction scheme, tin. So "the notion of a long-run demand curve is in the nature of a fiction." Gradual price rises do less damage (pp. 26–27).
5. **Dynamics and Hicks.** Random fluctuations slow adjustment. Progress makes each producer's demand lower and more elastic, so the forces that first kept price below the monopoly level later keep it *above* the optimum. Introducing the entrepreneur's subjective costs (Hicks) via "equal-profits" lines raises price (important for a one-man lawyer, less for joint-stock companies). "The best of all monopoly profits is a quiet life" (Hicks), so monopoly slows invention and progress (pp. 27–31).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Monopoly price** — the price that equates MC and MR on the (estimated) demand curve.
- **Demand surface / frequency curves** — the probability distribution of sales at each price.
- **Short-run vs long-run demand curve** — immediate response vs response after consumers adjust. The long-run curve is "a fiction" because price changes themselves shift it.
- **Equal-profits lines** — iso-income curves in price–output space; tangency with demand gives Robinson's result (Diagram V).
- **Don't confuse:** this is *not* Coase's "The Nature of the Firm" (Economica, Nov 1937, context, not in folder). Also, Coase questions Robinson's *behavioural* assumptions, not her *market-structure* concepts.
