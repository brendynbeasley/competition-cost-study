# 06 · Giddings (1887) — The Persistence of Competition
*Political Science Quarterly, 2(1), Mar 1887, pp. 62–78* · Group: A — Trusts, monopoly & the state · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** Giddings: competition is never destroyed, only converted. Pools curb only ruinous below-cost rivalry, so Ricardo still stands.

**Responds to:** #05 Clark, the article just before his in the same issue. Giddings uses Clark's grouping by stage of production and his reasons why a combination stays inside one group, and he supplies the empirical study of residual competition that Clark left to someone with "practical observation" (Clark p. 58). Also responds to #04 Hadley 1886, footnoted (p. 65) among the economists predicting that competition will be suppressed and wanting compulsory publicity; to Bagehot (capital will become perfectly mobile); and to Cairnes (non-competing groups)  
**Answered by:** #12 Bullock 1901 takes up the same persistence-of-competition theme with new evidence (it cites Giddings's later *Democracy and Empire*, not this article). #08 Marshall 1890 likewise notes that trusts fail when they charge too high a price

## Main claim
Combinations will grow, but competition will not be destroyed. It is a permanent force that combinations only redirect: into new products, better quality, and pressure from outside capital. Pools are unstable defensive bodies that rise and fall with the trade cycle. What they can suppress is only ruinous competition "below the solvency line" (p. 78). Normal Ricardian competition, which pushes price to cost of production, survives and is even strengthened.

## Argument in steps
1. Bagehot expected capital to become perfectly mobile. Since then pools seem to contradict him, and many economists (Hadley, Clark) now forecast that competition will be suppressed and replaced by arbitration and publicity (pp. 62–65).
2. Two errors to avoid: assuming competition is absent because it is not visible as on a produce exchange, and ignoring it because it is imperfect. Competition is "an implication of the conservation of energy," because people's energies are unequal. The combination equilibrium is unstable: members are unequal, some cheat, and outside capital keeps accumulating (pp. 65–67).
3. The facts bear this out (pp. 67–69). Prices have fallen since 1870 despite pools: an adult's daily supplies cost 43.53 cents in 1870 and 30 cents in 1885. Nail mills rose from 68 to 79 in 1883. The anthracite pool of 1873 broke in 1876. In the wall-paper pool, one member paid $10,000 a year in forfeits rather than stop underselling. Probably not one-tenth of the combinations limited competition for long.
4. Structure (pp. 69–73). Groups by personal qualification cut across Clark's groups by stage of production, and a combination forms inside one such group. Combination is easier among the few producers of final, specialized goods than among the many producers of crude materials, unless there is a natural or patent monopoly (anthracite, steel). Capital shut out of a pooled field finds new utilities (steel replacing iron) and better quality (the brass-goods pool competing on excellence).
5. Combinations are defensive and track depressions. Charging high prices invites new capital, and heavy fixed charges then make the resulting fight ruinous. So pools must let price "gravitate to an equality with cost of production" (p. 76). Ricardo never assumed chronic selling below cost; heavy fixed charges are a new fact. Curbing that waste increases *normal* competition (pp. 74–78).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Persistence of competition**: competitive energy is conserved; when blocked in one form it appears in another (new utilities, quality, outside capital).
- **Combination vs non-competing group** (p. 71): members of a non-competing group compete with each other but not with outsiders; members of a combination do not compete with each other but do compete with outsiders.
- **Primary / secondary / tertiary sub-products**: stages of production. Combinations are easiest at the final, specialized stage; shocks are passed backward toward crude materials and downward toward "automatic" labour.
- **Normal vs abnormal competition**: competition that drives price to cost (normal, to be kept) vs chronic below-cost warfare made possible by fixed charges and large reserves (abnormal, which pools rightly resist).
- **Don't confuse:** Giddings is *not* anti-combination. He accepts pools as defensive and even proposes "combination actuaries" to set fines for price-cutting (p. 77). He disagrees with Clark only about how far competition is actually suppressed.

## Likely exam angle
- Clark vs Giddings, published back to back in PSQ in March 1887. Same evolutionary framework, different emphasis: Clark says combination now limits competition and leaves residual forms; Giddings says competition persists and pools only stop below-cost warfare.
- An early empirical case that cartels are unstable (cheating, entry, cycles). Compare Marshall #08 on trusts that price too high and Bullock #12 on independents reappearing.
