# 65 · Burns (1937) — The Organization of Industry and the Theory of Prices
*Journal of Political Economy, 45(5), pp. 662–680* · Group: E — Imperfect vs monopolistic competition · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** Industry studies should start from prices and trace where the "disintegrated" entrepreneur's functions have gone.

**Responds to:** Theories of imperfect competition "resting ultimately upon the theory of monopoly" (Chamberlin, Robinson; context); the older "Trust and Corporation Problems" literature; antitrust, Robinson-Patman Act, NRA (context); Berle–Means-type separation of ownership and control (context)  
**Answered by:** No direct reply in the set. Given at the same AEA Chicago meeting (Dec 1936) where parts of Chamberlin #63 were presented. Machlup #69 (1939) takes up the value and difficulty of empirical case studies.

## Main claim
Price theory built on "competitive individualism" (the entrepreneur, the competitive market, the rational buyer) no longer fits industry. New theories of imperfect competition need far better data. Industry studies should take *price behaviour* as their central object and analyse it through the new *distribution of functions*: the disintegration of the entrepreneur, the scale of production relative to markets, integration, and the transfer of the appraisal of utilities from buyer to seller (sales promotion).

## Argument in steps
1. Knowledge of how industry operates is in a "sad state." Law and policy (antitrust, Robinson-Patman, NRA) rest on outdated theory, and old industry studies described technique and organisation but ignored price policy (pp. 662–664).
2. The central objective should be "the analysis of the behavior of prices," aimed at improving general price theory (p. 664).
3. **Disintegration of the entrepreneur.** In the corporation, ownership and control are separated. Who now holds "control," and do they maximise income? Three relocations follow: (a) larger scale relative to markets, (b) reintegration of activities, (c) seller takes over appraisal of utilities (pp. 666–667).
4. **Scale.** Few firms must estimate elasticities, rivals' reactions and the threshold that attracts new investment. Markets have geographical and commodity dimensions (basing-point systems such as cement). Price discrimination; price leadership claims need scrutiny. **Integration** blurs costs through joint allocation, and contracts (resale price maintenance, tying, leasing) shift entrepreneurial functions (pp. 667–675).
5. **Sales promotion.** Sellers try to change demand, so "any tendency toward normal profits works out … through the raising of costs rather than the decline of prices." Style and quality competition. Price lines. A call for better price data (BLS) and organised research (pp. 675–680).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Disintegration of the entrepreneur** — the old entrepreneur's functions (risking capital, setting price/output) split among corporate actors and contracts.
- **Relocation of functions** — scale, integration, and transfer of utility appraisal to sellers.
- **Don't confuse:** Burns offers a *research programme*, not a new equilibrium model.
