## Main claim
Cournot's *Recherches* of 1838 was ignored for decades, yet it is the founding text of what is now a flourishing mathematical school. Its price chapters are "almost uniformly excellent" and largely unsuperseded: monopoly, duopoly, unlimited competition, complementary goods, taxes. But Cournot was careless in three places:
- his duopoly premise, that each seller treats the rival's output as fixed, is unrealistic;
- his tariff result rests on a mathematical slip;
- his "social income" chapters fail because he lacked the idea of consumers' rent.

## Argument in steps
1. **Occasion (pp. 119–121).** Bacon's 1897 translation. Mathematical economics has grown from almost nobody in 1838 to about thirty enthusiasts now. Cournot is the "principal founder". Fisher offers a reading guide: about 103 essential pages, plus calculus notes.
2. **Monopoly (pp. 124–125).** Cournot first drew the demand curve. In his §30, fixed charges do not affect monopoly price, a point now "conspicuous" in American railway experience (Hadley). A rise in marginal cost raises the monopoly price, sometimes by more than the rise and sometimes by less.
3. **Duopoly (pp. 125–127).** Cournot's price falls between monopoly and competition, but his premise that each assumes the rival's *output* is constant is wrong. If each assumes the rival's *price* is fixed, undercutting drives price to the competitive level (Bertrand, Marshall, Pareto, Edgeworth). Real duopolists are like chess players, so the problem is "dynamic". The best treatment is Edgeworth's.
4. **Competition and complements (pp. 127–128).** Under unlimited competition, price equals φ′(x), "marginal cost", so Cournot anticipated Jevons, Menger and Walras. He also drew supply and demand curves, later credited to Fleeming Jenkin (1870). On complements: a single monopolist of copper *and* zinc sets a lower brass price than two separate monopolists. Edgeworth applies this to railways, where merging connecting lines lowers fares.
5. **Errors and revival (pp. 129–138).**
   - The tariff formula fails the check that the effect should vanish when the tax does.
   - Social income leaves out consumers' rent: the triangle of loss.
   - The revival came through Walras, Jevons and Marshall.
   - Fisher closes by defending mathematics: wages are "a flow, and not a fund".

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Cournot's duopoly assumption**: each producer takes the rival's output as given.
- **Bertrand's alternative**: each takes the rival's price as given, which ends in competitive pricing.
- **Marginal cost**: Fisher's verbal name for Cournot's φ′(x).
- **Complementary monopoly (joint demand)**: copper and zinc used in fixed proportions to make brass.
- **Consumers' rent**: Marshall's consumers' surplus, missing from Cournot's income chapters.
- **Don't confuse:** Fisher calls Cournot's complementary-monopoly *conclusions* "in the main consonant with facts" and objects only to the motives assumed. Moore #15 calls the same conclusions "extraordinary".
