## Main claim
Railways are the leading type of "public works characterised by monopoly" that justifies state intervention. Before analysing their rates, the "laws of return" must be stated precisely. Returns can be increasing or diminishing in two senses:
- the **primary** (marginal) sense, which compares successive increments;
- the **secondary** (average) sense, which looks at product per unit of outlay measured from zero.

The average sense suits competition. Under monopoly, and under a state railway that maximises consumers' surplus, the primary sense is what matters: "the greater of these is the primary" (p. 362).

## Argument in steps
1. **Plan (pp. 346–349).** Five sections, laid over Mill's five books:
   1. laws of return and cost;
   2. value under monopoly;
   3. **duopoly** (Einaudi's term);
   4. incidence of taxation under monopoly;
   5. state regulation.
   This file contains Section I only.
2. **Definitions (pp. 349–351).** A "law" can mean a concept or a proposition. The provisional definition uses successive equal doses. In Carver's table, increasing returns end at the third dose, where the increment peaks at 120 bushels.
3. **Size of dose and the two species (pp. 353–356).** The answer depends on the size of the dose. Extra carriages, extra trains and a new track each give a different answer. Edgeworth gives a general definition, then splits it into primary and secondary species, which can disagree. By the average test, Carver's increasing returns last until the fourth dose, not the third.
4. **Which sense for which regime (pp. 358–362).**
   - *Competition*: averages matter, and external economies come in, so the secondary sense applies.
   - *Monopoly*: the secondary sense is "even fallacious", because a monopolist maximises the amount of profit, not the rate of profit.
   - *State monopoly* maximising consumers' surplus: the primary sense applies.
5. **Several factors and discontinuity (pp. 362–370).** With several factors, testing returns by scaling all factors in proportion (a : 1) is misleading. Firms can take different paths to the maximum. Some inputs change only in jumps, like the steps of the Great Pyramid: carriages, then trains, then tracks. A big fixed outlay followed by variable outlay gives "Increasing Return *par excellence*". That explains why railway net receipts rise more than in proportion to gross receipts (Ripley).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Primary definition**: returns are increasing when a second dose adds more product than the first. This is the marginal test, and it is the criterion for a maximum.
- **Secondary definition**: returns are increasing while average product per unit of outlay rises.
- **Duopoly**: "two (and not more than two, or at most very few) dealers competing" for a numerous public (p. 348).
- **Coefficients vs factors of expense**: unpaid adjustments (train times, where porters stand) versus paid inputs.
- **Don't confuse:**
  - This instalment is about laws of return, not yet the dispute over joint cost versus discrimination. It only *mentions* that "Joint Cost (for freight and passenger) and Discrimination" make concrete examples hard to find (p. 361 n.). Edgeworth's explicit verdict for Pigou is in #19.
  - It also predates Pigou's *Wealth and Welfare* (1912).

## Diagram
Fig. 1A (p. 352) is a cost curve.
- Axes: product z on the horizontal; outlay x on the vertical.
- The curve starts at L on the outlay axis, which is the fixed outlay.
- Up to **P** returns are increasing in the primary sense.
- **Q** is where a tangent drawn from the origin touches the curve. Secondary (average) increasing returns end there.
- **R** is the competitive optimum, where the tangent is parallel to the price line OS.

Under competition only the stretch beyond Q matters. A monopolist may stop between P and Q, or even below P.
