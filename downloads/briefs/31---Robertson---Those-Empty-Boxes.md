## Main claim
Pigou's twin-curve apparatus makes the boxes harder to fill and invites wrong policy. It lumps together different kinds of "decreasing cost". It also invents "external diseconomies" to explain increasing cost. Rightly understood, competition commits no "rigid mathematical sin" in either case. The case for permanent bounties to decreasing-cost industries and taxes on increasing-cost industries falls apart.

## Argument in steps
1. Terminology: say "increasing/decreasing cost" for industries; keep "diminishing return" for the law of variable proportions (pp. 16–17).
2. Two kinds of decreasing cost. **Class I**: lumpy fixed capital, like the medal die (£20 die + 5s a medal, from Cunynghame), or railways and newspapers. **Class II**: improvements in technique and organisation over time, as output grows (pp. 17–18).
3. Class I: the last medal is not the "net product" of the last 5s of running resources, because the fixed plant shares in it. Pigou's rule implies a nationalised industry should be subsidised for "the whole burden of the charges of the fixed original plant". That is absurd; it should cover total costs, imitating competition (pp. 21–22).
4. Class II: Pigou must treat all such economies as external, ignoring internal ones. Yet Marshall's representative firm captures internal economies. A National Guild that owned all the economies would still set output so receipts cover total costs. The marginal-supply-price curve has "no message" here, and the right policy is only a *temporary* infant-industry subsidy (pp. 22–26).
5. Increasing cost: Pigou needs "external diseconomies", but "who in the world are they?" The only real cause is diminishing return from land, and higher rents are transfers, as Young showed. Pigou's view that industries get extra land at the old rent conflicts with Marshall's hop-grower (pp. 26–28).
6. **Pigou's reply** (pp. 30–31): he is dropping the "duplex curve apparatus" from the new edition. His conclusions are Marshall's (*Principles* V.xiii). The subsidy reductio is a "grotesque misunderstanding". Young's criticism "is... substantially valid as regards long-period problems", and his book will be changed. **Robertson rejoins**: his quarrel is with the reasoning, not the geometry.

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Increasing/decreasing cost** — Robertson's preferred names for industries where average full cost per unit rises or falls with scale.
- **Class I vs Class II decreasing cost** — lumpy fixed capital spread over more units (happens at once when demand rises) versus gradual improvement over time.
- **External diseconomies** — Robertson's label (he says "I am not attempting to father the actual phrase on Professor Pigou") for the force Pigou's increasing-cost case needs.
- **Don't confuse:** Robertson enters the Clapham–Pigou fight only indirectly. He attacks Pigou's *apparatus* from the inside, not because facts are missing. Knight #30 makes a similar rent point, but wrote before seeing Robertson.
