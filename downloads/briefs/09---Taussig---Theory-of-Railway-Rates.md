# 09 · Taussig (1891) — A Contribution to the Theory of Railway Rates
*Quarterly Journal of Economics, 5(4), pp. 438–465* · Group: C Railway rates & joint cost · Tier: 1

**Hook (memorize this line):** Railway rates follow from joint cost, like wool and mutton. They are not Cohn's ethics of ability-to-pay.

**Responds to:** Gustav Cohn, *Die englische Eisenbahnpolitik* (1883), who says rates are like taxes set by *Leistungsfähigkeit* (ability to pay), so the state should own railways (summarised pp. 438–439; the book itself is not in your folder); Hadley, *Railroad Transportation* (each item should bear "its share of the fixed charges"); Seligman and the ICC on "value of service"; Sax's split into general and special costs; Mill, Bk III ch. xvi, on joint cost  
**Answered by:** Pigou, *Wealth and Welfare* (1912), ch. xii (context, not in your folder), then Taussig's defence #17 (Feb 1913), then Pigou #18 (Aug 1913); Edgeworth #19 (1915) takes Pigou's side

## Main claim
Most railway cost is joint: either for all traffic or for large groups of it. That covers the return on capital and most operating expenses. Meanwhile the services face demands of very different intensity. So, as with any joint products, demand has a lasting effect on relative rates. Freight classification and "charging what the traffic will bear" therefore come out of ordinary value theory, not out of a special ethical principle. In Taussig's words, railways are "extreme, but not anomalous" (p. 465).

## Argument in steps
1. **Cohn's view.** Fares are like taxes and follow the payer's means. Rate-making is therefore an ethical question, and railways should be publicly owned or regulated (pp. 438–440).
2. **Capital costs.** Return on capital takes 40–50% of gross receipts. In the long run it sets the *general level* of rates, but it plays no part in any *particular* rate: any traffic that pays more than its separable cost is worth carrying (pp. 441–443).
3. **Joint cost.** This is Mill's joint-cost case: each service contributes to the joint cost according to its demand (canal and turnpike tolls show the same pattern). Most operating expenses are joint as well: maintenance of way, stations, switching, repairs. Sax estimates that about three-quarters of all expense does not depend on individual traffic items. Fink's cost "per ton-mile" figures "represent no real thing" (pp. 443–453).
4. **Classification.** Coal and ores have price-sensitive demand, so they get low rates. Silks and dry goods will come even at high rates, so they pay high ones. The German "natural" (car-space) tariff, which ignored this, failed (pp. 454–456). Even so, "what the traffic will bear" has **two senses**: one from joint cost and one from monopoly (p. 457).
5. **Consequences.**
   - "Reasonable" rates are very hard to define.
   - Even a state railway run on "tolls" would still classify traffic. Here Taussig disagrees with Hadley.
   - Cohn's ethics "may be brushed aside": nobody thinks rump steak is priced out of mercy to the poor (p. 461).
   - "Value of service", read as exchange value, is circular (pp. 457–463).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Joint cost**: cost incurred for several services together that cannot be assigned to any one of them. Here it means the plant and most operating outlay, common to "the traffic as a whole".
- **Separate (separable) cost**: the floor. No traffic is carried for less than its own separate cost.
- **Charging what the traffic will bear**: fitting rates to demand. It has two senses: joint cost and monopoly (p. 457).
- **Leistungsfähigkeit**: Cohn's ability-to-pay (taxation) principle.
- **Value of service**: the ICC's and Seligman's phrase. Taussig thinks it is useful only as popular shorthand.
- **Don't confuse:**
  - Taussig does not deny monopoly. Any one rate can reflect both monopoly and joint cost. His claim is that joint cost would explain classification *even under free competition*.
  - When he calls coal's demand "small", he means sensitive to price, not small in quantity (p. 454).
