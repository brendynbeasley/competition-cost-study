# 50 · Robinson (1934) — Euler's Theorem and the Problem of Distribution
*Economic Journal, 44(175), pp. 398–414* · Group: E — Imperfect vs monopolistic competition · Tier: 2

**Hook (memorize this line):** The adding-up problem was never a problem: profits are zero, positive or negative as returns are constant, diminishing or increasing.

**Responds to:** Wicksteed's Co-ordination of the Laws of Distribution 1894 (context); J. B. Clark, Edgeworth, Pareto, Walras, Hobson, Marshall's "shepherds" footnote, Wicksell, Hicks's Theory of Wages (all context); Kaldor #49 (on entrepreneurship as a factor, p. 406 n.)  
**Answered by:** #59 Kahn 1935 builds on pp. 409–414 (the entrepreneur earns more than his marginal product under imperfect competition). #63 Chamberlin 1937 attacks the identification of entrepreneur with firm (pp. 578–579). (Compare #53, Kaldor's August 1934 review, on hired factors vs entrepreneur's remuneration.)

## Main claim
Euler's theorem and economic reasoning agree. Under perfect competition each factor is paid the value of its marginal product, and the product is exactly exhausted when returns to the firm are constant, which is the case when profits are zero. Generally, profits are zero, positive or negative as returns are constant, diminishing or increasing. Under imperfect competition the same holds in *value and outlay* terms. At the industry level, entrepreneurs receive *more* than their marginal physical product, a sign that firms are "uneconomically small."

## Argument in steps
1. **History of the adding-up problem.** Clark: no specific entrepreneurial contribution in statics. Wicksteed (1894) used Euler and assumed constant physical returns. Pareto, Walras and Edgeworth objected. Wicksteed's subtle error was to define factors so finely (each plough a factor) that marginal products vanish. Hobson and Marshall were "each right in what they assert, and wrong in what they deny" (pp. 398–404).
2. **Wicksell's solution.** With zero normal profits, competitive equilibrium is at minimum average cost, where physical returns to the firm are constant. With positive normal profits, diminishing returns prevail just enough to give that profit (pp. 404–408).
3. **Industry level.** The entrepreneur's marginal product *to the industry* = the firm's output minus what its factors would produce if dispersed among other firms = his reward. With economies of large-scale industry, factors get less than their marginal product to the industry (pp. 408–410).
4. **Imperfect competition.** Factors earn the marginal product in value (MPP × MR). Zero profit with falling average cost means *constant returns in value*. Measured in outlay (where factor supply is imperfect), profits are zero, positive or negative as returns in value and outlay are constant, diminishing or increasing: "The harmony between the economist and the mathematician is complete" (pp. 410–413).
5. **Industry with imperfect competition.** Employed factors receive less than MPP valued at price, so entrepreneurs receive *more*. The ratio of entrepreneurs to other factors is too high and firms are uneconomically small. Wicksteed glimpsed this (pp. 413–414).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Adding-up problem** — does paying each factor its marginal product exactly exhaust the product?
- **Perfect competition (Robinson 1934, p. 405 n.)** — infinite elasticities of demand for output *and* of factor supplies to a single firm, with "no reference to free entry … or normal profits" (compare #57).
- **Constant returns in value/outlay** — a proportional rise in outlay on every factor raises the value of output in the same proportion.
- **Don't confuse:** marginal product *to the firm* (meaningless for an indivisible entrepreneur) vs *to the industry*.
