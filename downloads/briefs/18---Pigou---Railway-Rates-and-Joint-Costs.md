# 18 · Pigou (1913) — Railway Rates and Joint Costs (with Taussig's comment)
*Quarterly Journal of Economics, 27(4), pp. 687–694 (Pigou pp. 687–692; Taussig's reply pp. 692–694)* · Group: C Railway rates & joint cost · Tier: 2

## Main claim
The two authors largely agree in practice. Where a railway would *not* pay under uniform mileage rates, discrimination is justified. Pigou reaches that through the theory of monopoly; Taussig through joint cost. The real dispute is about lines that *would* pay under uniform rates. Pigou says their services are not joint, because the overheads are "common costs only". Under free competition rates per ton-mile would then be equal, so uniform mileage rates should be the ICC's ideal, apart from special cases. In his reply, Taussig says joint cost applies whenever the most profitable traffic alone cannot fill capacity. He also says the common/joint distinction is not vital to the question.

## Argument in steps
1. **Framing.** This is a policy question for the ICC: the "value of service" principle, or uniform rates per ton per mile. Pigou compares it to protection versus free trade (pp. 687–688).
2. **Agreement.** If differential rates are what make a line profitable, they are desirable. Pigou adds a reservation in favour of state bounties. In the sub-case where even the cheapest possible railway cannot fill its capacity, joint cost and monopoly are "equally suitable avenues of approach" (p. 688).
3. **The narrowed dispute.** Pigou admits jointness between A→B and B→A traffic (back loading) and a few minor cases. Otherwise "Taussig sees jointness everywhere, and I see it nowhere". For the sake of argument he concedes that copper transport and coal transport are two things (p. 689).
4. **Three arguments (pp. 690–692).**
   - (i) *Analogy*: cotton mills and steelworks also carry heavy overheads, yet nobody calls batches sold to different markets "joint".
   - (ii) *Analysis*: the supply price of cotton seed depends on two variables, seed output and the price of fibre. Copper transport depends on one, total transport. A railway can carry more copper without carrying more coal, so these are **common costs, not joint costs**.
   - (iii) *Direct argument*: competing sellers move supply into the higher-priced market until prices are level, however large the overheads are.
5. **Conclusion and reply.**
   - Pigou's conclusion: uniform mileage rates are the ideal where a line pays under them. "Value of service" has a stronger claim in the sparsely settled Western states than in the Eastern (p. 692).
   - Taussig's reply: American rate complexity began in pioneer conditions and now survives partly through tradition. Joint cost applies when capacity cannot be filled by the most profitable traffic (New Haven's passengers, then freight). Electricity sold by night and by day, back loading and a mail car on a passenger train are joint even though none is "necessarily" produced (pp. 692–694).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Joint costs vs common costs (Pigou)**:
  - *Joint*: you cannot raise output of one product without raising the other (cotton fibre and seed).
  - *Common*: shared overheads, but outputs can be varied separately (copper and coal transport).
- **Uniform mileage rates**: equal rates per ton per mile. This is the cost-of-service ideal.
- **Supplementary expenses**: overheads, as opposed to prime costs.
- **Discriminating monopoly**: Pigou's preferred explanation for departures from uniform rates.
- **Don't confuse:** Pigou is *not* against all discrimination. He endorses it where a line would not otherwise be built: pioneer or Western conditions.
