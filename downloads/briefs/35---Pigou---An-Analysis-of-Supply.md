## Main claim
To get an industry's supply curve when individual firms are growing and shrinking, Pigou invents the **equilibrium firm**: a firm that is itself in equilibrium whenever the industry is. In a many-firm industry the supply price must equal both the marginal cost and the average cost of that firm. If its costs depend only on its own output, the industry has constant supply price. Decreasing supply price needs costs that depend on industry output. Once "transfer elements" (factor-price rises that merely redistribute) are removed, increasing supply price is impossible, except for imported materials from one country's point of view.

## Argument in steps
1. Defines normal supply price and the laws of decreasing, constant and increasing *supply price*. These terms avoid the average/marginal ambiguity of "returns" or "cost". Spontaneous changes (those that would happen at constant output) are excluded (pp. 238–239).
2. The equilibrium firm: like Marshall's trees in a forest, firms rise and fall but their tendencies cancel, so we may imagine one firm always in equilibrium. Unlike the representative firm, it need not be of typical or average size (fn., p. 240) (pp. 239–240).
3. Three cost cases: F(x) (own output only); F(x) + (x/y)Φ(y) (internal plus external costs, e.g. bought materials); F(x, y) (general). Marginal *additive* cost (others' output fixed) versus marginal *substitute* cost (industry output fixed); Pigou uses the latter (pp. 241–243).
4. Equilibrium: p = marginal cost (else the firm expands or contracts) and p = average cost (else firms enter or leave). Stability needs rising marginal cost, i.e. the minimum of a U-shaped average cost curve. Case 1 → constant supply price. Case 2 → supply price can rise or fall with raw-material prices (raw cotton). Case 3 → any law is possible (pp. 243–250).
5. Transfer elements: changes in factor prices of the form a·dp₁/dy + b·dp₂/dy are not a net cost to the community, only transfers to factor owners. Corrected for these, decreasing supply price is "likely" in many manufacturing industries (specialisation: Chapman on English cotton). Increasing supply price is impossible, because a 1% rise in all factors cannot give less than 1% more output (pp. 250–254). Marginal supply price (corrected) is above, equal to or below supply price under increasing, constant or decreasing supply price. Maximum satisfaction is where demand = corrected marginal supply price (pp. 256–257).

## Key terms & distinctions
- **Equilibrium firm** — a firm in equilibrium whenever the industry is. It need not be average-sized.
- **Marginal additive vs marginal substitute cost** — the extra cost of one more unit if others' output is unchanged, versus if industry output is unchanged (rivals contract).
- **Transfer elements** — the part of a rise in supply price that is only a higher payment to factor owners (Young's and Knight's rents), not a social cost.
- **Don't confuse:** the equilibrium firm (Pigou, a logical device) vs the representative firm (Marshall, typical size and normal ability; criticised by #34 Robbins).
